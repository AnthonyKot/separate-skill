How this is put together

Method

Almost all Dota advice is assertion from rank: I am Immortal, therefore do this. It might be right. There is no way to tell, and no way to notice when it stops being right.

This book is an attempt at the other thing. Every figure in it is registered in a file, and a script re-checks every registered figure against the record it came from.

What happens next depends on the kind of figure, and the distinction is worth stating because collapsing it would overstate the whole method. A figure with a fixed source — a line in a patch note, an event in a specific match — either still matches its record or does not, and when it does not the build goes red. A figure drawn from a sample of live play is recomputed from the sample committed alongside it, which is also gating; but comparing it against the game as it stands today can only be advisory, because a population that has moved is not an error. It is the thing this book most wants to notice.

Where the numbers come from

Four classes of source, ordered by distance from the game itself. The order matters more than the list, because it decides what may be said with what.

ClassWhatUsed for
Official recordValve's patch datafeed, dated and versionedRules. Quoted directly
Machine-derived match recordParsed match JSON — events, purchase and buyback logs, ward logs, per-minute gold and XP, the full draftWhat happened in a specific game
Direct observationThe replay itselfWhat a careful viewer can see. Described as observation, never as measurement
InterpretiveCommentary, analysis, recollection, bracket consensusProposing a reading. Never a number

The second class is deliberately not called primary. It is Valve's replay put through somebody else's parser, and that is a real distinction worth keeping.

Two things this book refuses to do

Win rate is not advice. A hero's win rate is confounded by who picks it and what they are picking into. It can describe a population. It cannot instruct you. Any guide that reads a percentage off a stats site and turns it into a recommendation has skipped the only interesting step.

A professional match cannot tell you what your bracket does. Watching five coordinated professionals is useful for seeing the shape of a decision, and useless for establishing what usually happens in your games. Claims about the Archon–Legend bracket are sourced from samples of ranked Archon–Legend games. Where a pro match is used, the chapter says what transfers and what does not — the failure has a name here, the altitude error, so that it can be caught.

What a green build proves, and what it does not

It proves that every registered figure is present in the record it is attributed to, checked by something that could actually see that record. That is all.

It does not prove the advice is right. The previous book in this series shipped a chapter in which every single claim verified and the central causal arrow pointed backwards — the argument said one thing caused another when the dates in its own quoted sources ruled that out. Every row passed. Nothing in a checker can catch that.

So the machinery is a floor, not a warrant. It stops the book asserting numbers it has not looked up. Whether the reasoning built on those numbers is any good is a question for a reader, which is why corrections are wanted.

Four ways a sample lied, before a word was written

The bracket data is supposed to be the honest part — real games at the reader's own rank rather than borrowed professional examples. Getting it turned out to be four separate traps, each visible only after the previous one was fixed. They are worth stating, because the first sample looked entirely reasonable and was nonsense.

  1. Length. The public match feed is ordered by match ID, which is assigned when a game starts, and only finished games are in it. So the newest entries are the games that ended soonest. A fifty-minute game begun at the same moment has not arrived yet. The first sample had a median of 18.6 minutes and, in five hundred games, none longer than 25.7.
  2. Mode. Filtering by rank does not filter to ranked. That sample was 58% Turbo — which is the same bias wearing a second hat, since Turbo games finish fastest and so crowd the front of the feed.
  3. Clustering. Fixed both of those, and every game in the sample had started within the same minute. That is not a sample of a bracket; it is one time of day, one day of the week, one regional mix.
  4. Drift across patches. Spread the sample over five days, and one window landed 108 days back — quietly mixing games played under three earlier patches into a sample labelled with the current one. This was caught by a diagnostic added for tidiness rather than suspicion.

Corrected, the same bracket reads a median of 39.8 minutes with roughly half of games running past forty. The biased version said 18.6. Both were produced by the same short program, and only one of them was wrong in a way anyone would have noticed by reading it.

None of those figures appear in the book yet. The corrected sample has not been taken — the rewrite ran into a rate limit — and no bracket claim is registered until it has been.

Corrections

Wanted, especially about the Dota. The script checks that quoted figures are real. It cannot check whether a case supports the lesson drawn from it, whether a principle survives at your bracket, or whether a chapter is describing a patch that has since moved. If something here is wrong, open an issue.

Everything is a plain static site: hand-written HTML, one stylesheet, one small script, no build step, no framework, no trackers, no fonts or scripts loaded from anywhere else. Caster transcripts are used as research and never published here; they belong to the people who made them.
