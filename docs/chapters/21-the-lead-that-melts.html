Part V · Chapter 21 · Can you convert advantage into a win?

The Lead That Melts

It is thirty-eight minutes. You are twelve thousand gold ahead. You have taken two lanes of barracks and you are standing outside their base for the fourth time, waiting for someone to move first.

Every time you walk in, one of you dies and the rest come out. Every time you go back to farm, they take a lane back. The number at the top of the screen still says you are winning, and you have started to notice that it says the same thing it said ten minutes ago.

What actually happened

The same thing, at the highest level of play available, with every timestamp on record.

Stop here and commit before reading on

It is 39:39. You are Yakult Brothers. Everything below is in the record at this moment; nothing below is hindsight.

YOU (Radiant) Lifestealer · Earth Spirit · Lion · Brewmaster · Winter Wyvern THEM (Dire) Spectre · Centaur Warrunner · Lich · Clockwerk · Snapfire 38:25-39:23 fight ended 16s ago — you lost 2 (Earth Spirit, Lion) they lost 3 (Centaur, Clockwerk, Snapfire) 38:36 Roshan killed · 38:37 aegis DENIED — nobody holds it 37:04 your Winter Wyvern has already used buyback 38:55 their Clockwerk has already used buyback 39:39 last barracks down · MEGA CREEPS both their tier-four towers still standing gold +12,380 xp +989 (gold peaked at +19,106 two minutes ago)

You have mega creeps and they have lost three heroes in the last minute. Write down four things before you read what happened:

  1. The objective. What is the next attempt meant to buy — the fort, a specific hero dead, both tier-fours, or time?
  2. The acceptable cost. As a quantity. How many of you may die for it, and which ones?
  3. The stopping condition. What would you have to see to turn around?
  4. The missing information. Of everything you cannot see, which would change your answer most?

Your answer can be right and this game can still go badly, and it can be wrong and go well. That is the nature of the decision, and it is why the commitment is graded rather than the result.

What happened next

Both tier-four towers fell, at 40:09 and 40:22. A fight began at 40:13, in the middle of that: three of Yakult Brothers died and none of Rune Eaters did.

By the minute-41 sample the experience lead had gone negative — −2,705, having been +4,934 one minute earlier — and at minute 42 it was −3,124. The team holding every barracks in the game was now the lower-levelled team. Rune Eaters bought back at 43:12, 45:57 and 46:09.

The fort fell at 46:21: six minutes and forty-two seconds after mega creeps, and nine minutes after the gold lead peaked. Yakult Brothers won. The gold lead at minute 45 was +11,252, having shed 7,854 from its peak.

Two details deserve to be pulled out, because they are the ones a scoreboard hides.

The first: the lead peaked at 37:00, and the fight that reversed it had started at 36:47. The number looked its best thirteen seconds after the trade that began undoing it. A lead is reported at the moment it is banked, not at the moment it is earned, and the two are never the same moment.

The second: the fight at 40:13, immediately after both tier-four towers came down. Three of the winning team died and none of the defenders did. That fight happened with mega creeps already walking into the enemy base — that is, at the exact moment the advantage was supposed to be overwhelming.

The reasoning

It helps to be strict about what the record establishes and what it does not, because this is a chapter about a number that means less than it appears to.

Observed. Every timestamp above. The barracks, the towers, the buybacks, the deaths in each fight, the gold and experience curves minute by minute.

Supported inference. The advantage was converted into structures efficiently and into a finished game slowly. Between the last barracks and the fort, the winning side lost more heroes in fights than it killed, and its experience lead went negative while holding a permanent creep advantage.

Hypothesis. That defending a base with nothing left to lose is a strong position: shorter walks from the fountain, high ground, and an opponent who must come to you. The record is consistent with this. It does not prove it.

Unknowable. Why any of it was chosen. Whether the fight at 40:13 was a decision or an accident, whether anyone intended to siege at that moment, what was said. Five people made those choices and the record contains none of their reasons.

What the sequence shows is a distinction the scoreboard is not built to display. A lead is a stock. Closing is a flow. Gold, experience and buildings accumulate: once you have them, you have them, and the number stays where it is while you do something else. Ending a game is not like that. It is a series of fights that must be won on the opponent's ground, and each one is a fresh event that your accumulated advantage improves but does not decide.

This is why buildings are a misleading measure of progress. They are the easiest thing a lead buys, because a building does not reposition and cannot buy back. Taking all six barracks proves the lead was real. It does not move you closer to the win in the way the map suggests.

An advantage does not close a game. It buys repeated permission to try to close one — and each attempt is paid for in a currency the scoreboard does not show: your cooldowns, your positions, your respawn timers against theirs. A team that treats the lead as the achievement will keep spending attempts at the moment the number looks best, which is precisely the moment it has already begun to be wrong.

Why that final exchange becomes so expensive — what high ground does to a fight, and what buyback does to a lead — is chapter 22. Whether conditions permit an attempt at all is chapter 23. How to sequence the ending once they do is chapter 24. This chapter has the job that comes before all three, and it is the one nobody performs: noticing.

The diagnostic. From the moment your team takes its first set of barracks, ask one question on a loop — is my lead still growing, or has it stopped converting? Two observable answers, both available mid-game: whether the gold difference has moved in the last three minutes, and whether the last attempt bought anything you could name in advance. A lead that has stopped growing while the attempts continue is the state this chapter is about. What to do about it is the next three chapters; recognising you are in it is this one, and a player who never notices will not reach for any of them.

Where this stops applying. This is an argument about conversion, not an argument for haste. Nothing here says to force the fight earlier: the same record shows a team losing three heroes for none at 40:13 while attempting exactly that. It also does not apply to a lead that is still being built — before the first barracks, taking space and farming is the conversion, and Part III is about that.

It applies specifically to the state in the situation above: your advantage is large, it has stopped growing, and the buildings you can take cheaply are already taken.

What this case cannot show, and no other case here does yet. Yakult Brothers won. More than nine minutes after the lead peaked, with almost eight thousand of it gone and an experience deficit, but they won. So this chapter demonstrates that conversion stalls; it does not demonstrate that stalling loses.

What is owed is a match with the same shape — barracks taken, lead peaked, repeated failed attempts — that ends the other way, so the cost of the stall can be stated rather than implied. Until that case exists, the honest claim is the narrow one: a large lead and every barracks in the game bought this team nine more minutes of work, and could have bought worse.

What transfers, and what does not. This is a professional match, and the chapter is for a player queuing alone. That difference has to be spent honestly rather than waved at.

Transfers: buildings are the cheapest thing a lead buys and the weakest evidence of progress; the final fight is fought under the defender's best conditions; buyback lets a losing side refuse your best moment.

Does not transfer: almost every action in the timeline. Denying the aegis one second after Roshan dies, five players arriving together, buybacks timed to a siege — these assume coordination that a solo queue does not supply. If you read this chapter as a plan for what your team should do next, it will fail, and it will fail in a way that looks like your teammates' fault. The transferable part is a way of reading your own replay, which is why the actions below are things you do alone.

Whether this stall is common in Archon–Legend games is not established by this chapter. One professional match cannot show that, and no bracket sample in this book has been registered yet.

Next game

  1. When your team takes a set of barracks, note the clock. That is the start of the conversion, not the end of the game.
  2. Before each attempt on their base, name what it is meant to buy and the most you will spend on it. If you cannot name the purchase, take the wave instead and make the next attempt the named one.
  3. Track which defenders you have seen buy back, and whether enough time has passed for that option to have returned. You cannot know who currently holds the gold — so treat the ones you have not seen as available.
  4. After you die inside their base, do not walk back by the same route on reflex. Check whether the vision, the position or the cooldown that killed you has changed. If none of the three has, nothing about the attempt has changed either.
  5. After two failed attempts, stop repeating the same entry. Name the next enabling condition you personally can create: push the lane nobody is pushing, put vision on the entrance you keep dying at, keep your own buyback available, or wait out one named cooldown.

After the game

Open the replay at the minute your gold lead peaked. Your client draws the graph; find the maximum and put a timestamp on it.

The observable decision: the first attempt on their base that you made after that peak.

The pass mark, set before you look: before that attempt you had named an objective and a maximum acceptable cost — and either the attempt achieved the objective within that cost, or you disengaged at the stopping condition you had stated.

Written out, it looks like this:

Objective: force their last two buybacks Acceptable cost: one support death, no cores Stop if: our first core drops below half before any of them dies

Note what is not being graded. Dying is not automatically a fail: a support who dies to force two buybacks has bought exactly what they named. Surviving is not automatically a pass — walking away from a won fight having taken nothing you can name is a failure with a tidy scoreboard, and it is the one this chapter is really about. You are grading the commitment, not the result, because the result depends on four other people and the commitment does not.

One replay, one timestamp, one question. If you review nothing else this week, review the first attempt after your best number.
