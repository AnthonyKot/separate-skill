Part VII · Chapter 30 · Which of this changes for your seat?

Position Three

It is minute fourteen and you are ahead. Not the team — you. You have earned more gold than the carry you have spent fourteen minutes standing in front of, and for the moment more than anyone on your own side, including the player whose farm the entire draft was built to protect.

In about two minutes that will no longer be true, and it will keep not being true for the remaining twenty-seven. That much this chapter can show you. What it cannot show you — and what the rest of it is about — is whether the change is a verdict on anything you did.

What actually happened

Team Syntax vs Ilbirs Esports · Asgard Championship · match 8926599506 · patch 7.41e

Forty-three minutes and fourteen seconds, Radiant won, seven teamfights. What makes this game worth the chapter is that it contains both offlanes — two players in the same seat, in the same match, under the same patch, both of whom were ahead of the enemy carry they were laning against at minute ten.

Take the Radiant one first. At minute 10 the offlane hero had earned 4,477 gold and 47 last hits. The enemy safe-lane carry facing him had 2,736 and 34. That is a gap of +1,741 gold and +13 last hits against the hero whose farm the opposing draft exists to guarantee.

It goes further than the lane. From minute 6 through minute 14, that offlane hero had earned more than anyone else on his own five — more than his mid, more than his carry. Meanwhile his side was 2,161 gold behind overall at minute 14. The highest-earning player was on the poorer team, and he was the offlaner.

Which "gold" this is, because it changes what the numbers mean. Every per-hero figure in this chapter is cumulative gold earned — the running total a hero has taken in since the horn. It is not the gold in their pocket, and it is not quite net worth either. This offlaner's line reads 5,793 at minute 14 while he was carrying 1,440 unspent at the end of the game and had already committed a large part of it to a Blink Dagger two minutes earlier. The distinction matters twice over: it is a different quantity from the one the post-game screen shows you, and it is not a number any player can read off their screen mid-game for anyone, including themselves. Everything below is replay-derived, and the exercises at the end are built accordingly.

He had spent some of it. A Blink Dagger completed at 12:20. He had also died twice, at 4:32 and 10:49.

Stop here and commit before reading on

It is 14:00. You are the Radiant offlaner. Everything below is in the record at that moment, and nothing below it is.

YOU 5,793 gold · 67 last hits · Blink finished at 12:20 · two deaths THEM the carry you are laning against: 5,400 gold · 53 last hits LANE GAP +393 gold, +14 last hits four minutes ago it was +1,741 and +13 TEAM you have the highest gold of your five GAME your side is 2,161 gold behind overall

Commit in writing, before reading on:

  1. The objective. What the next three minutes are meant to buy — stated in a sentence that does not contain the word gold.
  2. The acceptable cost, as a quantity, and it has to follow from what you named in the first answer. Zero is a valid answer and may be the right one. Cost is also not only deaths — a minute of farm, a cooldown you will want at minute sixteen, tower health, an item revealed early, or a part of the map you stop contesting while you are gone are all things you can spend, and three of them are cheaper than dying.
  3. The stopping condition — the observable event that means this has stopped buying anything.
  4. The missing information — what you would most want to know and do not.

Three answers all pass. One is an objective on the map. One is hold, keep farming, and here is the event that would change my mind — inaction with a stated condition is a decision, and the seat is not obliged to spend on a schedule. The third is the answer this chapter was written around: these two numbers do not establish that I am ahead in any way that matters, and here is what would settle it. If you take that one, name the further information. That is the whole of it.

What does not pass is keep doing what I am doing without a stopping condition, because there is nothing there to be right or wrong about later.

That table is not empty — it is a real account of what each player accumulated, and pretending otherwise would be its own kind of overclaim. What it cannot do is narrower and more awkward: read it alone and you cannot grade either player's decisions, and you cannot reliably say which side won. It does not say the ten deaths were the better play; this book bans grading a decision by what came after it, and would be breaking its own rule in a seat chapter if it made an exception here. It does not say the tidier row was worse. The row is evidence. It is insufficient evidence, which is a different complaint and the one worth having.

The reasoning

Observed. Every figure above: both minute-ten gaps, the rank series from 1st at minute 6 to 4th at minute 42, the sign change at minute 16, the team gold deficit at minute 14, both final rows, the two tower times, the fight at 16:27, and ten death timestamps.

Supported inference. For both offlaners in this game, the one quantity the record keeps about the seat — distance ahead of a single named opponent — was largest before minute fifteen and negative afterwards. And across the two of them, the final row does not order with the result.

Hypothesis. That the early advantage was converted into something. Something did happen: the same team's carry finished on 32,900 net worth and 500 last hits with two deaths in forty-three minutes, and did 10,690 damage to buildings, the highest in the game. Set that beside an offlaner who died ten times and it forms an attractive story. It is a story. The record contains no positions, no lane pressure, no attention, and no counterfactual, so it cannot connect one row to the other, and this chapter does not.

Unknowable. Whether either offlaner played well. What any other line would have produced. What either of them intended, at minute fourteen or at any other minute.

So the chapter cannot tell you that the deaths bought the carry's farm, and it is not going to imply it with a well-placed paragraph break. What it can establish is narrower and more useful:

A statistic can stay accurate long after it has stopped being sufficient. The gap against your lane opponent does not expire. It goes on being exactly what it says it is. What changes is the question it can answer.

Early, while both of you are standing in the same lane taking from the same creeps, the difference is a reasonably clean description of one thing: how that lane went. Once the lanes break, the same subtraction keeps producing a number and that number is still correct. It has stopped being a grade. A shrinking gap is consistent with having wasted the advantage, with having converted it somewhere the record cannot attribute to you, with farm deliberately handed to a teammate, and with the two heroes entering the economic shapes their draft always expected. The row cannot distinguish those. In this game it does not even try.

So the instruction is not stop looking at it. It is narrower and harder: stop asking it a question it can no longer answer. The seat's characteristic mistake is not farming too much, which is what the genre warns about. It is continuing to use a still-accurate number as a verdict, because it is the most legible thing on the screen and it used to be one.

The direct comparison is not unique to this seat: the mid lane is measured against its opposite number in the same way, and rather more famously. What the record shows here is narrower — that for this offlaner the comparison held a large value early and had reversed by minute sixteen. A support has no comparable number at any point in the game, which is the contrast chapter 32 was written from.

What this seat changes

Part VII asks one question of each seat: how does it change what is scarce, what is visible, and which presumption you start from? Position three's answers:

AxisPosition three
What is scarce Gold still matters here and this seat still needs its item timings — anyone telling you otherwise is selling something. What is unusually scarce is the period in which an early lane advantage can still be converted, before the two cores' economic paths separate and the comparison stops describing a shared situation.
What is visible Early on the record holds an unusually clean proxy for one part of your job — the resource difference between you and the opposing safe-lane core. It is a proxy, not a measurement: it does not see tower pressure, lane control, rotations you forced, or an area you denied. A support has nothing comparable at any point, which is the contrast this chapter is drawn against.
Which presumption A falling rank inside your own five is compatible with this seat being played well and with it being played badly, so on its own it carries very little. What needs explaining is never the decline itself; it is what, if anything, happened elsewhere while it was declining.

Note which chapters this hands back to rather than re-teaching. Whether an advantage must be spent at all belongs to chapter 15, and detecting that an accumulated advantage has stopped producing progress is chapter 21's job in the endgame. This chapter's exclusive claim is smaller and about the seat: which two numbers describe you, and when they stop.

Where this stops applying. The fifteen minutes are this match, not a law. Drafts that leave the offlane farming safely, or that put the carry into a losing lane, move the crossover in either direction and there is no registered evidence here for a typical value. Read your own crossover; do not adopt this one.

It does not apply to a lane you are losing. Everything above starts from being ahead at minute ten, and a player who is behind is answering a different question — one this book puts in Part II rather than here.

And the professional gap is the wide one in this chapter. Ten deaths from an offlaner in a coordinated team can be paid for by four players who know what to do with what those deaths buy. In solo queue nobody has agreed to that trade, and a reader who takes the death count as permission has read this chapter backwards.

What this case does not establish. Anything at all about Archon–Legend offlanes. The bracket sample behind this book is drawn from OpenDota's public match feed, which reports duration, rank tier and result and no per-hero data of any kind — so there is no registered evidence here about when a bracket offlaner's lane advantage decays, whether it decays, or what any of them do about it. An inversion would look like this: a stated bracket at which holding the early lane advantage longer produces the better result, and holding it the same length produces a worse one. Nothing here can test that, and the block stays empty rather than being filled with reasoning.

What transfers, and what does not.

Transfers: the reading procedure — the two numbers that describe this seat, the fact that they expire, and the discipline of looking somewhere else once they have. So does the two-offlane comparison, which is a fact about what a row can report.

Does not transfer: minute sixteen, the ten deaths, the item timings, and the shape of the decay. Those are one game between five coordinated professionals and five others.

Next game

  1. At minute ten, judge the lane from what you can actually see — their level against yours, the items they have completed, the tower's health, whether either of you can stand in the lane safely. Commit to one word: ahead, even, or behind. One word, before you have any figures. The figures come later and they are the check on your judgement, not a substitute for it.
  2. Name what that judgement is for, in a sentence that does not contain the word gold — a tower, a camp you take away from them, a rune you can contest, a rotation you can now survive.
  3. Before you go, say the maximum cost out loud, and say what pays it. Zero is a valid answer and is often the right one. Cost is not only deaths: it can be a minute of farm, a cooldown you will not have for the next fight, tower health, an item you reveal early, or a part of the map you stop contesting while you are away.
  4. Then the stopping condition — the observable event that means this has stopped buying anything and you leave.
  5. After the game, in the replay, check the one-word judgement you made at minute ten against what the record shows. You are calibrating a reading you have to make live, from information you will actually have. Over ten games you will learn whether you habitually read the lane as better or worse than it was, and that is worth more than any single game's figures.

Why none of these tells you to read the enemy's numbers. An earlier draft of this list opened with at minute ten, write down your gold and the enemy carry's. That exercise cannot be done. The quantity this chapter is built on is cumulative gold earned, which is a replay figure — no player reads it live for an opponent, and no player reads it live for themselves. Whether any in-client feature exposes some related figure for enemy heroes is a question about the current client that this book has not verified against a primary source, and it does not need to: the number the chapter uses is not that number either. So the live exercise is built from things you can see, and the record is used where it belongs, afterwards.

After the game

Open the replay at minute fourteen. Not at a death and not at a fight. In this game that is the point where a +1,741 difference had narrowed to +393 and the next sequence began — it is a transition in one match, not a threshold this book has established. Nothing here says minute fourteen is when an offlane advantage runs out. Watch the three minutes after it without deciding in advance that they were the last valuable ones.

The observable decision: what you did with those three minutes.

Two marks, and they are separate on purpose.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. Before acting you had named an objective that was not your own gold, a maximum acceptable cost, and a stopping condition. You pass if you achieved the objective inside the cost, or disengaged when your stated condition occurred. Holding deliberately with a stated condition passes on the same terms.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. Given what was knowable at the time: was that objective worth that cost, and was the stopping condition sensitive enough to fire before the cost was paid? This is not hindsight — it does not ask what happened, it asks whether the plan was reasonable on the information you had. Without it the first mark is self-validating, because a plan reading objective: their tower; cost: three deaths; stop: after three deaths passes every time it is followed.

So the review lands in one of four places rather than passing or failing: good process, good calibration; good process, poor calibration — you did exactly what you declared and it was not worth declaring, which is the cell a single mark cannot see; no process, so there is nothing to grade; or not enough evidence to reconstruct what you knew, which is a real answer and not an evasion. Whether it worked is written on a separate line and never moves the grade — least of all in the third case, which is the one most easily awarded a quiet pass.

Objective: their bottom tier-one, or the camp behind it Cost: one minute of my own farm; no death, no cooldowns I need at 16:00 Stop if: their mid appears on my half of the river Missing: whether their support has bought a sentry yet

What is not on the pass mark: your net worth, your rank in your team, and the result. All three are available and all three are the outcome, which chapter 01 grades nothing by. The two offlaners above make the case better than an argument can — one of these rows won the game and the other did not, and you cannot tell which from looking.

Do this once a game for ten games. What you are building is not a verdict on any single one; it is a record of whether you habitually spend the window, hold it past its expiry, or have never noticed it was there.
