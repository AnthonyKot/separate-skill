Part I · Chapter 05 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

The Death You Should Have Taken

Four chapters have now taught you to interrogate your deaths. You have a classification, an information boundary, a way to segment a sequence, and a procedure for pricing an exit.

What you also have, whether or not anybody intended it, is a number to get down. And the number is easy to get down. You do not have to play better to reduce it. You can reduce it by playing less.

This chapter is the one that stops the previous four from making you worse. It is here because a book about avoidable deaths, read by somebody who was already avoiding too much, gives them a rigorous vocabulary for the thing that was losing them games.

And it cannot show you the death of its own title, which is the point rather than an apology. No professional case can. A death that should have been taken is an action that did not happen, and the record has no entry for actions that did not happen — no timestamp, no position, no column. So the case below demonstrates why the omission is invisible, and the death in the title has to come from a decision you record yourself, in your own game, at the time. The exercise at the end is built for exactly that.

One of those rows is tidier than the other. Four fewer deaths, more than twice the net worth, five levels ahead. If you were handed both at the end of a game and asked which player had the better time of it, the question would not feel difficult.

A small disagreement, before the large one. Alchemist's scoreboard reads three deaths; the kill log credits a hero with two. Across the eight matches this book has committed, the two counts differ in four of them, by anything from −1 to +3, because the kill log records only deaths a hero is credited with and the scoreboard counts every death however it happened. The number you have been taught to interrogate does not entirely agree with the log of who killed you.

Stop here and commit before reading on

You have the two rows above and nothing else. Commit in writing:

  1. Which of those two rows would you rather have finished with? One sentence, and give the reason rather than the row.
  2. Name one cost that both rows may be hiding. Not a vague one — something you could go and measure. The reveal adds one such cost; yours may be a different one and still be right.
  3. What would you need in order to price the difference between them?

These two lines do not let me choose is an admissible answer and possibly the best one — if you take it, say what is missing. So is preferring either row, provided the reason is stated. There is nothing here you cannot answer from what is on the page, and the reveal will not grade whether you picked the professional's row.

Four fewer deaths, and a hundred and six seconds longer spent dead. The tidier row accumulated more than twice the dead time.

The available timings are consistent with later deaths costing more. Undying's are early — 3:21, and six of the seven before minute nineteen. Alchemist's two hero-credited deaths are at 11:05 and 26:06. A death late in a game costs more than a death early in one; the exact relationship is a rule this book has not looked up and will not assert from memory.

And the third death cannot be placed at all, which is the same argument again. Alchemist's scoreboard says three; two are timestamped because a hero was credited with them. The third was dealt by a neutral creep — the record names it, and gives no time, because nothing that anybody gets credit for killed him. So the 187 seconds cannot be fully accounted for from the timings shown, and this chapter does not pretend otherwise: one of the three intervals is invisible. A death with no killer to credit is a death the log has no row for.

So the first thing: the count and the dead time are different quantities, and on the same team in the same game they disagree by a factor of five per death.

Be careful what that licenses, because the trap here is the one this chapter is about. Dead time is not "the real cost" either. It is one direct, recorded cost. It does not price the gold lost, the map access surrendered, the objective that fell while you were gone, a buyback you were forced into, or what you had already achieved before dying — nor whether the seconds landed in a window that mattered. So 187 against 81 does not establish who paid more overall. It establishes that the number on the scoreboard is not the only number, and neither number is a grade. Replacing deaths with dead time would be this chapter making the mistake it was written to prevent.

Neither played badly, incidentally — they won, both contributed, and Alchemist finished on more than twice the net worth.

The reasoning

Observed. Both scoreboard lines; both dead-time totals; both net worths and levels; the death timings; the scoreboard-against-kill-log discrepancy in four of eight committed matches.

Supported inference. That a death count and the time those deaths cost can disagree, on the same team in the same game, and by a factor of five per death. That the asymmetry here follows from when the deaths happened, which the timings show.

Hypothesis. That level and net worth drive the difference as much as the clock does. Very likely, and not separable from these two rows — Alchemist was five levels and seventeen thousand gold ahead, and any of those could carry the effect.

Unknowable. Whether either player should have taken more risk or less. What any avoided risk would have produced. And — this is the important one — anything at all about how common passive play is, at any bracket. This book has no evidence on that and this is the chapter most tempted to pretend otherwise.

Now the second thing, which is larger than the first and cannot be shown with a table, because the whole point is that it is not in the file.

The scoreboard raises no debit for a risk you declined. It prices some of what a death costs. For the fight you did not join it creates no entry at all — and while the consequences may surface elsewhere, in a lost objective or a teammate's row, the option you weighed and the reason you rejected it appear nowhere.

Think about what that does to anybody optimising the visible side. Declining costs you nothing that the instrument will ever charge you for. The consequences can be severe and they will be recorded against something else — a tower, a teammate, a game — never against the decision. So the feedback you get for over-avoiding is not weak. It is absent, and pointed the wrong way: every avoided risk improves the number you can see.

That is not a claim that you are doing it. It is a claim about the instrument, and it is the same claim the rest of Part I has been making about other instruments:

ChapterThe instrumentWhat it cannot register
01The death countWhich kind of death any of them was
02The replay's informationWhich of it you could have obtained at the time
03The fight windowWhether it contains one event or several
04The fight's recorded valueWhat the path you did not take would have cost
05The whole scoreboardWhich risks you considered and declined, and why

Which is why the free-death test in chapter 01 is written the way it is — and why its mirror has to be written carefully, because the obvious mirror is wrong.

The obvious mirror says: you fail if an exchange was available and you declined it. That is far too permissive, and it would teach the reckless inverse of the error this chapter exists to prevent. Exchanges are available constantly. Most of them are not worth their price, and declining a bad one is a correct decision that this test must not punish.

A risk is wrongly declined when, given what was reasonably knowable at the time, the expected exchange justified its cost — and what you did instead offered less.

Two clauses, and both are load-bearing. The exchange has to have been worth its expected price, which is the same standard chapter 01 applies in the other direction. And the alternative counts: farming a safe camp is not nothing, and a decline that bought a real item at a real timing may beat a fight that was genuinely available. The test is a comparison, not a presence check.

Stated that way it is a harder question than was something happening, and the difficulty is the point. It also means the scoreboard is unhelpful in both directions: a player failing this test has a tidier row for the failure, and a player passing it may have a worse one.

The target of Part I was never zero deaths. It was avoidable ones, and the distinction has been doing quiet work in every chapter since. A player who takes no risks is not passing this test. They are failing it in the direction that photographs well.

Where this stops applying. This is not permission. Nothing here says take more risk, and a reader who leaves with I should die more has inverted the chapter as thoroughly as one who leaves the first four with I should die less. Both are the same error: replacing a decision with a target.

It is also not chapter 04 again, and not chapter 20. Chapter 04 prices an exit from a fight you are in. Chapter 20 will price a specific fight you decline. This chapter is about neither moment — it is about the metric, and the way a season of reviewing one number bends what you do in every moment.

And two rows are two rows. One pair, one game, one patch. The asymmetry here is real and the size of it transfers to nothing.

What is owed, and it is the largest debt in Part I. The foundations of this book describe two stuck players — one who dies too much and one who dies too little — and that split came from a review pass that cited no usable data. It is a hypothesis. There are no thresholds behind it, no bracket evidence, and nothing here establishes that either population exists in the proportions anybody imagines. It is kept because the failure mode it names is real enough to design against; it is not kept because it has been shown. Any sentence in this chapter that reads like most players do this would be inventing, and there is deliberately not one.

What would settle it: a bracket-stratified sample with per-player fight participation and risk-taking measures. The sample behind this book carries duration, rank tier and result and no per-hero data of any kind, which is itself a registered claim.

Next game

  1. When you die, read the respawn timer before anything else and say the number. That is one immediate cost this death imposed — not the whole of it — and it is on your screen for as long as you are looking at it.
  2. At the end of the game, add those numbers up and count the deaths. Two figures, one game, and they will not tell the same story. Neither of them is your grade; having both is what stops either becoming one.
  3. Once per game, name a risk you declined — a fight, a ward, a contest, a rotation — and say in one sentence what declining bought you. If the honest answer is nothing, I did not want to die, you have found that the decline was unexamined — which is not the same as wrong. The risk may have been terrible. Whether it was is the replay's question, below; whether you asked is this one.
  4. When you are about to avoid something, ask which of the two questions you are answering: is there an exchange available here, or will this make my row look worse. They give different answers surprisingly often, and only the first is a decision.
  5. Do not set a death target for a game. Not a low one and not a high one. A target replaces the question with a number, which is the whole failure this chapter exists to prevent.

After the game

Use the risk you named in item three — the one you declined. Without it the honest result is not enough evidence to reconstruct my process, and here that matters more than anywhere else in Part I. The replay does preserve a great deal: the fight you did not enter, how close you were, where you went instead, what you farmed, what was visible. What it does not reliably preserve is the option you weighed and the reason you rejected it — and without the note, a known outcome will supply both, convincingly and falsely.

Open the replay at the moment you declined.

The observable decision: what you did instead, and what was available where you went.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. Before declining you could name what the risk was for, what it would have cost, and what you were doing instead. Pass if you can name all three. Declining deliberately passes; declining because you were not looking does not.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. On what was visible at the time: was an exchange available whose expected value justified its cost — and did it offer more than what you did instead? Both halves. Not did it work out, and not merely was something happening. That is chapter 01's test pointed the other way, and it is the only place in this book where the honest answer might be I passed on something I should have taken.

Declined: the fight at their tier two, minute 28 For: nothing I can name. I was close to an item and did not want to lose it Instead: farmed the safe triangle for ninety seconds Available: four of them committed, our initiator had blink — worth the risk? Instead-value: ninety seconds of safe farm, about half an item

Four results, as everywhere: good process and good calibration; good process and poor calibration — you declined deliberately and the exchange was there; no process, meaning you did not decide at all; or not enough evidence. Whether the game was won goes on its own line and never moves the grade.

Ten of these gives you a count within the ten you chose. What it is good for is noticing whether your reason for declining is ever anything other than the row.
