Part I · Chapter 05 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

The Death You Should Have Taken

Four chapters have now taught you to interrogate your deaths. You have a classification, an information boundary, a way to segment a sequence, and a procedure for pricing an exit.

What you also have, whether or not anybody intended it, is a number to get down. And the number is easy to get down. You do not have to play better to reduce it. You can reduce it by playing less.

This chapter is the one that stops the previous four from making you worse. It is here because a book about avoidable deaths, read by somebody who was already avoiding too much, gives them a rigorous vocabulary for the thing that was losing them games.

One of those rows is tidier than the other. Four fewer deaths, more than twice the net worth, five levels ahead. If you were handed both at the end of a game and asked which player had the better time of it, the question would not feel difficult.

A small disagreement, before the large one. Alchemist's scoreboard reads three deaths; the kill log credits a hero with two. Across the eight matches this book has committed, the two counts differ in four of them, by anything from −1 to +3, because the kill log records only deaths a hero is credited with and the scoreboard counts every death however it happened. The number you have been taught to interrogate does not entirely agree with the log of who killed you.

Stop here and commit before reading on

You have the two rows above and nothing else. Commit in writing:

  1. Which of those two rows would you rather have finished with? One sentence, and give the reason rather than the row.
  2. Name one cost that both rows are hiding. Not a vague one — something you could go and measure.
  3. What would you need in order to price the difference between them?

These two lines do not let me choose is an admissible answer and possibly the best one — if you take it, say what is missing. So is preferring either row, provided the reason is stated. There is nothing here you cannot answer from what is on the page, and the reveal will not grade whether you picked the professional's row. It grades whether the thing you named in question two turns out to be one of the things the record can actually show you.

Four fewer deaths, and a hundred and six seconds longer spent dead. The tidier row cost more than twice the time.

The timings say where it came from. Undying's deaths are early — 3:21, and six of the seven before minute nineteen. Alchemist's two hero-credited deaths are at 11:05 and 26:06. A death late in a game costs more than a death early in one; the exact relationship is a rule this book has not looked up and will not assert from memory, but the direction is visible in these two rows without needing it.

So the first thing the count is not: the count is not the cost. Two players, one team, one game, and the row that looks disciplined paid the larger bill. Neither played badly — they won, both contributed, and Alchemist finished on more than twice the net worth. The point is narrower and it is about the instrument: deaths and what deaths cost you are different quantities, and only one of them is on the screen.

The reasoning

Observed. Both scoreboard lines; both dead-time totals; both net worths and levels; the death timings; the scoreboard-against-kill-log discrepancy in four of eight committed matches.

Supported inference. That a death count and the time those deaths cost can disagree, on the same team in the same game, and by a factor of five per death. That the asymmetry here follows from when the deaths happened, which the timings show.

Hypothesis. That level and net worth drive the difference as much as the clock does. Very likely, and not separable from these two rows — Alchemist was five levels and seventeen thousand gold ahead, and any of those could carry the effect.

Unknowable. Whether either player should have taken more risk or less. What any avoided risk would have produced. And — this is the important one — anything at all about how common passive play is, at any bracket. This book has no evidence on that and this is the chapter most tempted to pretend otherwise.

Now the second thing, which is larger than the first and cannot be shown with a table, because the whole point is that it is not in the file.

The scoreboard is a ledger with one side missing. It prices some of what a death costs. It prices none of what an avoided risk costs — and that side is not merely unmeasured, it has no column, no log and no entry.

Think about what that does to anybody optimising the visible side. The fight you did not join is free. The ward you did not place is free. The tower you did not contest is free, the teammate you did not follow is free, and the risk you declined at minute thirty is free forever, because nothing anywhere will ever write it down. Every one of those may have cost the game, and every one of them scores zero.

That is not a claim that you are doing it. It is a claim about the instrument, and it is the same claim the rest of Part I has been making about other instruments:

ChapterThe instrumentWhat it cannot register
01The death countWhich kind of death any of them was
02The replay's informationWhich of it you could have obtained at the time
03The fight windowWhether it contains one event or several
04The fight's recorded valueWhat the path you did not take would have cost
05The whole scoreboardEvery risk you declined

Which is why the free-death test in chapter 01 is written the way it is. It asks whether a meaningful exchange was reasonably available and whether it was taken — and that question fails in both directions. A player who accepted risk with no exchange available fails it. So does a player who had an exchange available and declined it, and their scoreboard will look better for the failure.

The target of Part I was never zero deaths. It was avoidable ones, and the distinction has been doing quiet work in every chapter since. A player who takes no risks is not passing this test. They are failing it in the direction that photographs well.

Where this stops applying. This is not permission. Nothing here says take more risk, and a reader who leaves with I should die more has inverted the chapter as thoroughly as one who leaves the first four with I should die less. Both are the same error: replacing a decision with a target.

It is also not chapter 04 again, and not chapter 20. Chapter 04 prices an exit from a fight you are in. Chapter 20 will price a specific fight you decline. This chapter is about neither moment — it is about the metric, and the way a season of reviewing one number bends what you do in every moment.

And two rows are two rows. One pair, one game, one patch. The asymmetry here is real and the size of it transfers to nothing.

What is owed, and it is the largest debt in Part I. The foundations of this book describe two stuck players — one who dies too much and one who dies too little — and that split came from a review pass that cited no usable data. It is a hypothesis. There are no thresholds behind it, no bracket evidence, and nothing here establishes that either population exists in the proportions anybody imagines. It is kept because the failure mode it names is real enough to design against; it is not kept because it has been shown. Any sentence in this chapter that reads like most players do this would be inventing, and there is deliberately not one.

What would settle it: a bracket-stratified sample with per-player fight participation and risk-taking measures. The sample behind this book carries duration, rank tier and result and no per-hero data of any kind, which is itself a registered claim.

Next game

  1. When you die, read the respawn timer before you do anything else and say the number. That is what this death actually cost, and it is on your screen for as long as you are looking at it.
  2. At the end of the game, add those numbers up rather than counting the deaths. Two figures, one game, and they will not tell the same story.
  3. Once per game, name a risk you declined — a fight, a ward, a contest, a rotation — and say in one sentence what declining bought you. If the honest answer is nothing, I did not want to die, that is the finding, and it is the only one in Part I that will never appear on a scoreboard.
  4. When you are about to avoid something, ask which of the two questions you are answering: is there an exchange available here, or will this make my row look worse. They give different answers surprisingly often, and only the first is a decision.
  5. Do not set a death target for a game. Not a low one and not a high one. A target replaces the question with a number, which is the whole failure this chapter exists to prevent.

After the game

Use the risk you named in item three — the one you declined. Without it the honest result is not enough evidence to reconstruct my process, and here that matters more than anywhere else in Part I, because a declined risk leaves no trace whatever. Every other chapter can find its moment in the replay. This one cannot: you are reviewing something that did not happen, and if you did not write it down at the time it is gone.

Open the replay at the moment you declined.

The observable decision: what you did instead, and what was available where you went.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. Before declining you could name what the risk was for, what it would have cost, and what you were doing instead. Pass if you can name all three. Declining deliberately passes; declining because you were not looking does not.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. On what was visible at the time: was there an exchange available that you turned down? Not did it work out — whether a meaningful exchange was there. That is chapter 01's test pointed the other way, and it is the only place in this book where the honest answer might be I passed on something I should have taken.

Declined: the fight at their tier two, minute 28 For: nothing I can name. I was close to an item and did not want to lose it Instead: farmed the safe triangle for ninety seconds Available: four of them were committed; our initiator had blink up

Four results, as everywhere: good process and good calibration; good process and poor calibration — you declined deliberately and the exchange was there; no process, meaning you did not decide at all; or not enough evidence. Whether the game was won goes on its own line and never moves the grade.

Ten of these gives you a count within the ten you chose. What it is good for is noticing whether your reason for declining is ever anything other than the row.
