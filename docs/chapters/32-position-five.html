Part VII · Chapter 32 · Which of this changes for your seat?

Position Five

You finish the game 0/8/15 with the lowest net worth on the team. You won.

Nothing you did in that game is on the scoreboard. Not one thing. The scoreboard has columns for kills, deaths, assists, net worth, last hits — and you spent forty-six minutes producing something none of those columns can hold, which is why the person who typed at you in minute twenty had nothing to look at either.

This chapter is the seat where several of the earlier chapters stop being true as written. Before it does anything else, it has to ask the question Part VII opens on: are you actually playing the seat you queued for? A player who selects support and then farms like a core is not failing at any chapter in this book. They are answering a question they never noticed was asked.

What actually happened

Vici Gaming vs Team Liquid · 1win Essence II · match 8928118730 · patch 7.41e

Forty-six minutes and thirty-six seconds. On the winning side, a Crystal Maiden finished with 0 kills, 8 deaths and 15 assists, and a net worth of 16,558 against her own carry's 40,243 — about forty per cent of it.

She also placed 14 observer wards and 25 sentries. The observers whose removal is recorded contributed 3,964 ward-seconds of vision — a little over an hour of ward-time, in a game that lasted forty-six minutes. (Overlapping wards are counted separately there: it is ward-time contributed, not minutes of the game covered.)

And one number that turns out to matter more than any of those. Of the twelve observers whose end is recorded, eleven survived exactly 360 seconds — six minutes, to the second, over and over. They were not destroyed. They expired.

The twelfth is where this chapter happens.

Stop here and commit before reading on

It is 35:08. You are the Crystal Maiden, and you have gone out to ward. You have already put a sentry down; the observer is in your hand. Everything below is in the record.

YOU Crystal Maiden — net worth 10,100 at minute 35 YOUR CARRY Lone Druid, roughly three times your net worth THE GAME your team is ahead by 11,572 gold at minute 35 THEM Kez · Viper · Centaur Warrunner · Winter Wyvern · Keeper of the Light YOU HAVE died seven times already; this is minute 35 of 46 LAST ACTION placed a sentry, one second ago, on the same trip YOUR WARDS eleven of your last twelve observers lived their full six minutes

You are about to place the observer. Commit in writing:

  1. What is this ward for? A named thing it lets your team do — not "vision".
  2. How long do you need it to live for that to happen? Seconds or minutes, and it is allowed to be much less than six.
  3. What would tell you not to place it here — the observable thing that means walk away instead.
  4. What are you willing to pay? You are the cheapest hero on the map and this is your eighth trip. State the price you would accept for the ward being where you want it.

There is no trick in this one. The state above is what a competent support has in front of them, and every answer to question four is defensible except refusing to have one.

Twelve seconds, against a typical six minutes. One ward in twelve was contested at all, and that one was contested immediately — which tells you something was there, and that the placement had walked into it.

Ninety seconds after she died, two enemy tier-two towers came down.

The record does not say those things are connected. It says they happened in that order. A deep ward, a sentry, a dead ward, a dead support, two towers — the causal story writes itself and this book is not allowed to write it, because a replay contains no counterfactual and two towers falling has many candidate explanations. What can be said is narrower and still worth having: this is the shape of a position-five trade, and none of it appears in the 0/8/15.

The reasoning

Observed. The scoreboard line; the net worths; 14 observers and 25 sentries; eleven lifetimes of exactly 360 seconds; the twelve-second ward; the death at 35:39; the two towers.

Supported inference. A support's resources are time-boxed. Eleven identical six-minute lifetimes are the ward's full duration, so the value of a ward is fixed and known before it is placed, in a way the value of an item is not.

Hypothesis. That the deep trip and the death bought the towers. Attractive, unestablished, and exactly the kind of thing the record is worst at.

Unknowable. Whether any of her eight deaths was correct, what was being coordinated, and what she could see.

The seat changes which resources are scarce, and that is the whole of what Part VII is for. A core's scarce resources are gold and time. Position five's are different enough that several earlier chapters need re-reading:

Which earlier chapters invert, and how

This is the part only a seat chapter can do. Three chapters exist to test it against.

Chapter 01 — The Death You Didn't Notice — inverts at the default. Ch. 01 classifies a death as free when, given what was knowable, substantial risk was accepted with no plausible exchange available. That test is right for every seat. What flips is the presumption you bring to it. For a core, an isolated death with nothing visible around it is *presumed* worth investigating. For position five, the exchange is routinely a thing the record cannot show — a ward placed, a rotation forced, a carry not caught — so the same presumption produces a false positive nearly every time. Ch. 01 warns that a 0/12 line is not an accusation. This chapter is the reason that warning was written: the seat most likely to be misread by the scoreboard is the one whose product the scoreboard has no column for.

Chapter 12 — Every Item Is a Forecast — inverts in the strongest way, and it is almost funny. Ch. 12 asks you to name the window in which a purchase will first matter, and admits this is hard because the future is unknown. For position five, the ward tells you the window before you buy it. Six minutes, printed on the item. The forecast that ch. 12 has to coax out of a core is compulsory here: place a ward and you have committed to a claim about the next six minutes whether you thought about it or not. Eleven of these twelve wards ran their full duration, which means eleven forecasts were made and none of them was ever contested; the one that was contested was answered in twelve seconds.

Chapter 21 — The Lead That Melts — inverts in what conversion means. Ch. 21 says an advantage buys repeated permission to try to close, and that buildings are the cheapest thing it buys. For a core, converting means being present with the item that ends things. For position five, converting is the vision that lets the attempt happen at all, and it is spent before the attempt rather than during it. Your contribution to closing a game is usually complete before the fight that closes it starts.

Where this stops applying. Everything above is about a seat, not a hero, and the seat is defined by what is scarce rather than by which hero is on the screen. A Crystal Maiden with a large net worth in a long game is playing a different seat by minute forty, and the earlier chapters apply to her unchanged.

It also does not license dying. Nothing here says eight deaths is good, or that a support should trade freely because the scoreboard is unfair to them. Ch. 01's test is the test; what changes is the presumption you start from, and a presumption is not a verdict.

What is owed. A real inversion, in the strict sense: a case where advice from an earlier chapter, applied correctly by a position-five player, produced a worse result. The three above are re-readings — the earlier advice holds and its default changes — which is a boundary, not an inversion, and calling it one would repeat the error book 4 made three times in its first four chapters.

Also owed, and larger: this book has no evidence about position five at Archon–Legend at all. One professional support in one game establishes the shape of a trade and nothing about how common any of it is.

What transfers, and what does not.

Transfers: that the seat determines which resources are scarce; that the ward is a forecast with its window printed on it; that the scoreboard has no column for the product of this seat, so self-review from it is worthless here in a way it is merely unreliable elsewhere.

Does not transfer: the ward counts, the death count, and the deep trip at minute 35. A professional support wards into an enemy jungle with four teammates who know the plan. The state block above lists what she had; it does not list the thing that makes the trip reasonable, which is that someone was watching the map while she did it.

Next game

  1. Before each ward, say what it is for — a thing your team can then do, not "vision".
  2. Say how long you need it to live. If the answer is under a minute, place it somewhere you can walk away from.
  3. Count your own deaths in two piles as they happen: on a trip, and not on a trip. You will usually be surprised by the second pile, and that pile is the one ch. 01 is about.
  4. When you die, note whether the thing you went out for got done. Not whether it worked — whether it happened.
  5. Do not use the scoreboard to review this seat. It has no column for your game.

After the game

Open the replay at your last deep ward of the game. Not your first — your last, when the map was most dangerous and the decision hardest.

The observable decision: the moment you committed to the trip, which is earlier than the moment you placed the ward.

The pass mark, set before you look: before leaving, you could name what the ward was for, roughly how long you needed it, and what you would have accepted paying. Pass if you can name all three, whether or not the ward survived and whether or not you did.

For: the Roshan attempt we are about to make Need: ninety seconds, not six minutes Would pay: my life, if the ward is placed before I lose it Walk away: if two of them are already missing from the map

Then record one line separately: did the ward get placed? Not whether you survived, and not whether the plan worked — both of those depend on four other people. Whether the thing you left to do got done.

Ch. 01 grades a death by what was knowable when the risk was taken. Ch. 12 grades a purchase the same way. This grades a trip the same way, and the consistency is the point: the seat changes what is scarce, and never what counts as a decision well made.
