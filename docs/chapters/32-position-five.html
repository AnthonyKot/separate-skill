Part VII · Chapter 32 · Which of this changes for your seat?

Position Five

You finish the game 0/8/15 with the lowest net worth on the team. You won.

The scoreboard contains that line and almost nothing that would tell you whether you played the seat well. It has columns for kills, deaths, assists, net worth, last hits — and you spent forty-six minutes producing things none of those columns holds, which is also why the person who typed at you in minute twenty had nothing useful to inspect.

This chapter is the seat where several of the earlier chapters stop being true as written. Before it does anything else, it has to ask the question Part VII opens on: are you actually playing the seat you queued for? A player who selects support and then farms like a core is not failing at any chapter in this book. They are answering a question they never noticed was asked.

What actually happened

Vici Gaming vs Team Liquid · 1win Essence II · match 8928118730 · patch 7.41e

Forty-six minutes and thirty-six seconds. On the winning side, a Crystal Maiden finished with 0 kills, 8 deaths and 15 assists, and a net worth of 16,558 against her own carry's 40,243 — about forty per cent of it.

She also placed 14 observer wards and 25 sentries. The observers whose removal is recorded contributed 3,964 ward-seconds of vision — a little over an hour of ward-time, in a game that lasted forty-six minutes. (Overlapping wards are counted separately there: it is ward-time contributed, not minutes of the game covered.)

And one number that turns out to matter more than any of those. Of the twelve observers whose end is recorded, nine survived exactly 360 seconds and a tenth ran 361 — six minutes, to the second, over and over. They were not destroyed. They expired, and the record says so directly rather than by inference: an expiring ward is credited in the log to the player who placed it, and a destroyed one to whoever killed it.

Two were killed. One is where this chapter happens. The other is worth a line of its own — it was destroyed by Kez after 351 seconds, nine seconds before it would have run out on its own. Somebody spent an action on a ward with nine seconds left in it, which is a small and precise illustration of how little the fixed clock cares about anyone's plans.

Stop here and commit before reading on

It is 35:08. You are the Crystal Maiden, and you have gone out to ward. You have already put a sentry down; the observer is in your hand. Everything below is in the record.

YOU Crystal Maiden — net worth 10,100 at minute 35 YOUR CARRY Lone Druid, roughly three times your net worth THE GAME your team is ahead by 11,572 gold at minute 35 THEM Kez · Viper · Centaur Warrunner · Winter Wyvern · Keeper of the Light YOU HAVE died seven times already; this is minute 35 of 46 LAST ACTION placed a sentry, one second ago, on the same trip YOUR WARDS seven have ended so far; every one ran its full six minutes

You are about to place the observer. Commit in writing:

  1. What is this ward for? A named thing it lets your team do — not "vision".
  2. How long do you need it to live for that to happen? Seconds or minutes, and it is allowed to be much less than six.
  3. What would tell you not to place it here — the observable thing that means walk away instead.
  4. What is the maximum cost this purpose justifies? Zero is a valid answer, and often the right one — the price has to follow from what you named in question one. A ward for a vague future possibility does not justify what a ward for an objective starting in ninety seconds might.

There is no trick in this one. The state above is what a competent support has in front of them, and every answer to question four is defensible except refusing to have one.

Twelve seconds, against six minutes. Of the twelve observers whose ending is recorded, ten expired and two were destroyed — and this one was found immediately, which tells you something was there. (Two further observers were placed and were still standing when the game ended, so they have no recorded ending and are not in that twelve.)

Note also what six minutes does not prove. A ward that survives its full duration was not destroyed. Whether it saw anything, or enabled anything, is a different question the record does not answer.

Ninety seconds after she died, two enemy tier-two towers came down.

The record does not say those things are connected. It says they happened in that order. A deep ward, a sentry, a dead ward, a dead support, two towers — the causal story writes itself and this book is not allowed to write it, because a replay contains no counterfactual and two towers falling has many candidate explanations. What can be said is narrower and sharper: this is the shape a position-five trade leaves in the record — and it is also the shape two unrelated mistakes leave. A good trip that bought something, or a bad ward followed by an avoidable death, or towers that would have fallen anyway: the record holds the same six rows for all three. Nothing in the 0/8/15 distinguishes them either, which is why the decision has to be reviewed from the moment the trip began rather than from anything that happened after it.

The reasoning

Observed. The scoreboard line; the net worths; 14 observers and 25 sentries; nine lifetimes of exactly 360 seconds and a tenth of 361; ten expiries and two kills, taken from the log's own attribution rather than inferred from duration; the twelve-second ward; the 351-second ward; the death at 35:39; the two towers.

Supported inference. A support's resources are time-boxed. Nine lifetimes of exactly 360 seconds and a tenth of 361 establish the ward's full duration, so the value of a ward is fixed and known before it is placed, in a way the value of an item is not.

Hypothesis. That the deep trip and the death bought the towers. Attractive, unestablished, and exactly the kind of thing the record is worst at.

Unknowable. Whether any of her eight deaths was correct, what was being coordinated, and what she could see.

The seat changes which resources are scarce, and that is the whole of what Part VII is for. A core's scarce resources are gold and time. Position five's are different enough that several earlier chapters need re-reading:

What this seat changes about the earlier chapters

This is the part only a seat chapter can do, and it is worth saying what happened when it was attempted. The plan was to find where earlier advice inverts for position five. Tested against three chapters, nothing did. The decision rules held every time; what moved was what is scarce, what the record can see, and which presumption to start from. That turns out to be the more useful question, and Part VII now asks it instead.

Chapter 01 — The Death You Didn't Notice — inverts at the default. Ch. 01 classifies a death as free when, given what was knowable, substantial risk was accepted with no plausible exchange available. That test is right for every seat. What flips is the presumption you bring to it. For a core, an isolated death with nothing visible around it is *presumed* worth investigating. For position five, the exchange is routinely a thing the record cannot show — a ward placed, a rotation forced, a carry not caught — so the same presumption misfires here in a way it does not elsewhere. Ch. 01 warns that a 0/12 line is not an accusation. This chapter is the reason that warning was written: the seat most likely to be misread by the scoreboard is the one whose product the scoreboard has no column for.

Chapter 12 — Every Item Is a Forecast — inverts in the strongest way, and it is almost funny. Ch. 12 asks you to name the window in which a purchase will first matter, and admits this is hard because the future is unknown. For position five, the ward tells you the window before you buy it. Six minutes, printed on the item. The forecast that ch. 12 has to coax out of a core is compulsory here: place a ward and you have committed to a claim about the next six minutes whether you thought about it or not. Ten of these twelve wards ran their full duration, which means ten forecasts ran to term unchallenged. Two were contested: one answered in twelve seconds, and one taken with nine seconds left to run.

Chapter 21 — The Lead That Melts — inverts in what conversion means. Ch. 21 says an advantage buys repeated permission to try to close, and that buildings are the cheapest thing it buys. For a core, converting means being present with the item that ends things. For position five, part of the conversion happens earlier: establishing the information and access that let the attempt be made at all. That does not replace what you contribute during the fight, and reading it that way would be the most expensive mistake this chapter could cause.

Where this stops applying. Everything above is about a seat, not a hero, and the seat is defined by what is scarce rather than by which hero is on the screen. As a position-five player accumulates permanent resources, some of these default assumptions weaken — so reassess which resource is now genuinely scarce, rather than continuing to act as the poorest hero on the map out of habit. A number on its own does not change your seat.

It also does not license dying. Nothing here says eight deaths is good, or that a support should trade freely because the scoreboard is unfair to them. Ch. 01's test is the test; what changes is the presumption you start from, and a presumption is not a verdict.

What is owed. A real inversion, in the strict sense: a case where advice from an earlier chapter, applied correctly by a position-five player, produced a worse result. The three above are re-readings — the earlier advice holds and its default changes — which is a boundary, not an inversion, and calling it one would repeat the error book 4 made three times in its first four chapters.

Also owed, and larger: this book has no evidence about position five at Archon–Legend at all. One professional support in one game establishes the shape of a trade and nothing about how common any of it is.

What transfers, and what does not.

Transfers: that the seat determines which resources are scarce; that the ward is a forecast with its window printed on it; that the scoreboard has no column for the product of this seat, so the scoreboard is insufficient for self-review here in a way it is merely unreliable elsewhere — useful for finding moments to open, useless for judging them.

Does not transfer: the ward counts, the death count, and the deep trip at minute 35. A professional support wards into an enemy jungle with four teammates who know the plan. The state block above lists what she had; it does not list the thing that makes the trip reasonable, which is that someone was watching the map while she did it.

Next game

  1. Before each ward, say what it is for — a thing your team can then do, not "vision".
  2. Say how long you need it to live, then match the risk of the placement to the value and urgency of what it enables — not to the duration. A ward needed for ninety seconds may justify a worse position than one needed for six minutes, if what it enables is starting now.
  3. Count your own deaths in two piles as they happen: on a trip, and not on a trip. The second pile is the one ch. 01 is about, and it is the one worth reviewing first.
  4. When you die, note whether the thing you went out for got done. Not whether it worked — whether it happened.
  5. Do not review this seat from the scoreboard alone. Death timings and assists can point you at moments worth opening; they cannot tell you what happened at them.

After the game

Open the replay at your last deep ward of the game. Not your first — your last, when the map was most dangerous and the decision hardest.

The observable decision: the moment you committed to the trip, which is earlier than the moment you placed the ward.

Two marks, and they are separate on purpose.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. Before leaving, you could name what the ward was for, roughly how long you needed it, and what you would have accepted paying. Pass if you can name all three, whether or not the ward survived and whether or not you did.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. On what you could see before you left: was that ward worth the price you had agreed to pay for it? A trip priced at your own death for vision you wanted in general is a fully specified plan and a bad one. This is the question that keeps the seat's licence from becoming a licence — naming a purpose does not make the purpose worth dying for, and this chapter is one long argument that the record will not tell you either way.

Four results, and the second is the one this exercise exists to find: good process, good calibration; good process, poor calibration — you named all three and the trip was not worth its stated price; no process, nothing to grade; not enough evidence to reconstruct what you could see. Whether the ward survived stays on its own line, as below, and never moves the grade.

For: the Roshan attempt starting in about ninety seconds Need: ninety seconds of vision on their approach, not six minutes Max cost: nothing — if it is contested I leave and we start without it Walk away: if two of them are already missing from the map

Then record one line separately: did the ward get placed? Not whether you survived, and not whether the plan worked — both of those depend on four other people. Whether the thing you left to do got done.

Ch. 01 grades a death by what was knowable when the risk was taken. Ch. 12 grades a purchase the same way. This grades a trip the same way, and the consistency is the point.
