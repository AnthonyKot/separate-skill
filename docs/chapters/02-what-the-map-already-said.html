Part I · Chapter 02 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

What the Map Already Said

You die somewhere you had no particular reason to be, and the honest post-mortem is four words long: I didn't see them.

Which is true, and is not the question. The question is what you could have seen — not with better reflexes or a wider monitor, but from information that already existed, in the game, before you moved. Chapter 01 established that a death is graded by what was knowable when the risk was taken. This chapter is about finding out what that was, which turns out to be a real and partly answerable question rather than a rhetorical one.

What actually happened

Stop here and commit before reading on

It is 7:50. You are Mirana. You have this second placed an observer and you are standing next to it. The state below is split into two lists on purpose, and the split is what this chapter is about.

AVAILABLE WITHOUT LOOKING FURTHER — your own actions, your allies', the ordinary interface your team's last observer expired at 5:43 the one your mid placed at 6:14 was gone within half a minute the only observer your team now has is the one you placed a moment ago you are standing beside it, at 7:50, in the eighth minute IN THE COMPLETE RECORD — but not automatically in front of you at 7:50 Radiant has three observers standing two of them went down at 7:41 and 7:47, in the six seconds before yours Radiant has three sentries standing to your two

Commit in writing, using only the first list:

  1. The objective for your next thirty seconds.
  2. The acceptable cost. Zero is a valid answer and may be the right one; cost is also not only your death — it can be the ward itself, a minute of your farm, or a cooldown.
  3. The stopping condition — what you would have to observe to abandon it.
  4. Which items in the second list would have changed your answer, and how. This is the real question. Answer it before reading on.

Then two more, and they are what this chapter is for. Do not sort the list you were given — write your own.

  1. One fact you would most want, that a deliberate action could settle before you move. Name the action and what it costs you.
  2. One fact you would most want, that no action available to you could produce in time. Naming it is how you stop being graded on it later.

Every answer that names its reasoning is admissible — which is not the same as good, and the difference is deliberate. The reveal will not grade whether you matched the professional. It will grade whether your two facts went in the right buckets, and a plan can be perfectly well stated and still be wrong about that.

What happened next

Mirana died at 8:15, twenty-six seconds after placing the ward, to Nature's Prophet. It was her second death of nine.

Her observer was destroyed by Bane at 9:10. It had stood for 81 seconds.

Radiant's three ran 320, 361 and 361. Two of those expired; the third — the 320 — was killed by Mirana at 11:17, three minutes after the death in this chapter. That matters twice. It corrects a sentence an earlier version of this page carried, which called all three untouched; and it is the evidence against the reading this chapter would otherwise invite, that Dire were not contesting vision. They were. She killed two Radiant observers in this game.

How the record knows. An expiring ward is credited in the log to the player who placed it; a destroyed one is credited to whoever killed it. So expiry and destruction are a fact here rather than something inferred from a duration — which is how both this chapter and chapter 32 first got it wrong. Reading fate from lifetime looks safe and fails silently: chapter 32 had a ward killed after 351 of its 360 seconds, and no duration heuristic would ever have caught it.

Where the facts sort. The three you were shown, then the two worth generating.

Notice what the second list turned out to be. Every fact the record volunteered was unavailable, and the two that could actually have been acted on were not in it — you had to write them yourself. That is not a trick played on you; it is the ordinary shape of replay evidence. A match file is generous with global counts and silent about the specific, local, checkable question you needed answered before you moved.

Which is the practical form of the whole chapter. The replay is best at exactly the category you can never act on, and worst at the one you can. So reviewing from a replay without writing your own list is a reliable way to feel informed and improve nothing.

Now the part that matters more than the death, and it is a negative result. None of this classifies the death. Chapter 01's verdict on death A stands exactly where it stood: the record holds no position for it, no health, no cooldowns, nothing about where anyone was standing or looking. Everything this chapter has added is real, checkable, and still not enough.

So what has actually been gained? The thing chapter 01 said the test required and did not supply: an information state. Not she died at 8:15, but she died twenty-six seconds after placing her team's only standing observer, following seventy seconds in which her team had none at all, against opponents holding three. That is not a verdict. It is the input a verdict would need.

The reasoning

Observed. Every ward placement and ending, per side, with durations; the four standing counts at 8:15; the seventy-second gap; the 24-second and 81-second lifetimes; Mirana's nine deaths and the timing of the second.

Supported inference. That the vision available to the two sides at 8:15 was asymmetric, and substantially so. Ward counts are a floor on what a side could see and a ceiling on nothing — but three standing against one is a real difference in what was discoverable, and it is computable to the second from the record.

Hypothesis. That Mirana's ward being destroyed after 81 seconds means it was found deliberately, and that being found means somebody was watching. Plausible; also consistent with a sentry placed for another reason entirely. Note the direction the evidence does not run: a ward destroyed quickly is evidence of contest, which is not the same as evidence of a bad placement, and a chapter that read it as carelessness would be inventing.

Unknowable. Whether this death was avoidable. What Mirana could see. What she or anyone else intended. Whether either team knew any of what the paragraph above establishes — the record's knowledge is not the players' knowledge, and this chapter's whole subject is the gap between them.

The record holds an information state you can reconstruct, and it is not the information anybody in the game had. The useful question is not what the record knows. It is which parts of what the record knows were discoverable at the time, by someone who went looking.

Four categories, and sorting a fact into the right one is the whole method. An earlier version of this page used three, with a row called known — which this chapter's own evidence forbids, since nothing in the record establishes what anyone attended to. Available is not noticed. And the old middle row quietly held two different operations: finding out a fact, and shifting a probability.

The two middle rows are the ones worth keeping apart. A sentry can tell you a ward is there. A missing enemy tells you nothing whatever about wards — it tells you about a hero. Both are useful and they answer different questions, and a review that treats them as one thing will eventually grade you for failing to deduce something that was never deducible.

Almost every bad review of your own game collapses the second and third rows. You watch a replay in which the enemy positions are drawn on the map for you, you feel the obviousness of it, and you conclude you were careless — when a good half of what feels obvious was, at the time, unavailable to anyone standing where you stood. That feeling is not insight. It is the replay client showing you both teams at once.

Where this stops applying. Ward counts are not vision. A ward can stand for six minutes covering ground nobody walks on, and the record cannot tell the difference between that and a ward that decided the game. Three against one is an asymmetry in opportunity to see, and nothing more precise should be read into it.

The reconstruction is also only as good as the parse. Placements and endings are recorded; what a ward saw is not, and the map coordinates in the file are the parser's grid rather than game units, so this chapter deliberately quotes no distances.

And none of it applies to a game you did not play. For a professional match you can reconstruct the record's half and never the player's half. For your own game you have both, which is the only reason the exercise below is worth anything.

What is owed. Any claim about how often Archon–Legend games contain a seventy-second observer gap, which would need bracket evidence this book does not have — the public match sample carries duration, rank tier and result and no ward data of any kind, and that absence is itself a registered claim. So the gap is presented as a thing that happened once, at the professional level, and never as a pattern in your games.

Next game

  1. Once per game, before you walk into ground you do not control, say the sentence out loud: "the last thing I actually saw here was ___." If the blank is empty or older than a minute, you have found an information gap — not an answer. Then either reduce it, or decide the objective is worth acting under it and say which.
  2. When you die, note one thing before the respawn timer runs out: whether you had asked that question. Not whether you were right. Whether you asked.
  3. Keep track of your own team's last observer — when it went down and roughly when it ends. It is one useful live fact that requires guessing nothing about the enemy, which is rarer than it sounds.
  4. When a hero you were watching disappears from a lane, treat it as information rather than as nothing. It is the cheapest item in the discoverable column and it costs a glance.
  5. Do not invent an exact enemy ward count. It is unavailable and always will be. Do form provisional local beliefs — this route is watched, that one probably is not — and say the evidence and the uncertainty out loud when you act on one. The banned move is the confident global number, not the reasoning.

After the game

Use a death for which you made the in-game note from item two above. Without that note, the honest result is not enough evidence to reconstruct my process, and that is a real outcome rather than a failure — chapter 01's warning about reconstructing a classification from vague memory applies with more force to reconstructing a plan, because a plan is exactly the thing a known outcome will happily invent for you.

Open the replay at that death and wind back sixty seconds. The ten-minute mark and the sixty seconds are arbitrary standardisation — pick something fixed so that ten reviews are comparable, not because either number is correct.

The observable decision: the moment you committed to being where you died, which is earlier than the moment you died.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. Before moving, you had a objective, a maximum cost and something that would have turned you around. Pass if you can state all three now and they are the ones you actually held then, not the ones the replay has suggested to you.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. Now use the replay's advantage deliberately: list what was on the map that you did not have. Then sort each item into discoverable or unavailable — and grade only the first list. Was your objective worth its cost given the discoverable information? That is the question. The unavailable column is not evidence about you and it never becomes evidence about you, however obvious it looks in a replay that shows both teams.

Objective: contest the far camp before their support rotates Cost: the ward, and nothing else Stop if: their mid is not on the minimap when I start moving Discoverable: their offlaner had not been seen for 40 seconds Unavailable: an enemy observer covering the exact route

Four results, as everywhere in this book: good process and good calibration; good process and poor calibration — you did what you planned and the discoverable information said not to; no process, so nothing to grade; or not enough evidence to reconstruct what you knew. Whether you died goes on its own line and never moves the grade, because you are reviewing a death and so you did.

Then one more question, and it is the one that turns a review into a repair. For the single most important fact on your available list, walk down four rungs and find the one that broke:

RungThe questionWhat a break there means
AvailableWas it exposed to me at all? If no, stop. Nothing here is about you and the review is over
NoticedDid I see it when it happened? An attention problem. It is fixed by where you look and when, not by knowing more
RememberedDid I still have it when I moved? A holding problem. This is what the one-sentence habit above is for
Acted onDid it change what I did? A decision problem — the only rung this book's other chapters can help with

Four rungs, four different repairs, and they are as distinct as chapter 01's three classes of death. I didn't see them collapses all four into one sentence, which is why it has never once led anybody to a fix. And note what the record cannot do here: it can establish that a fact was available and it can never tell you which rung you fell off. Only you were there. That is the half of the reconstruction this chapter has been saying all along you have to supply yourself, and this is where you supply it.

Do this once a game for ten games. What you are building is a count of which rung breaks most often — which is the only number in this chapter that is about you, and the only one that says what to practise.
