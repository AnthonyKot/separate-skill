Part I · Chapter 04 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

Leaving Is a Skill

The fight is going wrong and you know it. There is a moment — you can usually feel it — when the thought arrives: I should get out of here.

And then you do not, and afterwards the lesson you draw is that you should have left sooner. That lesson is nearly useless, because it is not a decision. It is a wish. The decision has three parts and none of them is leave: what your exit is, what it costs to use, and the moment it stops being available.

Stop here and commit before reading on

Wind back to 49:49, the second the fight begins. You are Undying. Everything below is in the record at that moment.

CLOCK 49:49. The game has been going fifty minutes YOU a town portal scroll, and a blink you have not used yet THEM an aegis, taken at 47:39 — somebody on the other side has a second life MAP two of their barracks are already down; this is deep in their half FIGHT it starts now, ten heroes, and you are in it

You are not being asked whether to fight. You are in one. Commit in writing:

  1. What is your exit? Name the specific thing that removes you — not "run".
  2. What does using it cost? The item and its cooldown are the small part. Name the rest: your share of the fight, the position, what your team is left holding.
  3. When does it expire? The observable event after which that exit is no longer available to you. This is the one people never write down.
  4. What is the trigger? The thing you will see that means: use it now.

Two answers pass that people assume do not. My exit is that I have none, and I have priced that before engaging is a complete answer — plenty of heroes have no exit and the knowledge is worth more than the wish. So is I intend to spend my life here and here is what it buys, provided you name the purchase. What does not pass is an exit with no expiry, because an exit you have not dated is a feeling.

What happened next

Undying used the scroll. Undying used both blinks. Undying died at 50:15, twenty-six seconds into the fight.

Doom did the same arithmetic with a different inventory — scroll, blink, Shiva's — and died at 50:04, fifteen seconds in. Earthshaker used a blink and a force staff and died at 50:21. Dark Seer used two blinks and a wind waker and lived.

The exit was available, was paid for, and did not work. Three times. That is the first half of this chapter and it is the half nobody says out loud: leaving is an action with a failure rate, and a plan that treats it as a guaranteed escape hatch is not a plan.

So the question should he have left? has no answer here, and this book is not going to invent one. What the fight shows is narrower and it is enough: staying had a recorded value, and it was large, and it went to somebody who died.

The reasoning

Observed. The fight window and its seven deaths with timestamps; the two conflicting death counts; every item use inside the fight; every gold and experience delta; the aegis at 47:39; the barracks before and the tower at 51:12.

Supported inference. That an exit is not free and not certain. Three heroes spent seven movement-item uses inside forty-seven seconds and all three died — whatever those uses were for, they did not produce survival.

Hypothesis, and a weak one. That any of those items was used in order to leave. A scroll can bring a player in as easily as take one out. A blink is an entry tool at least as often as an escape. The record holds no destination and no intention, so every use of the word exit in this chapter describes a category of item, never a proven purpose. The argument does not need it: it needs only that the items were spent and the deaths happened.

Unknowable. Whether anybody should have left, or left earlier. What leaving would have cost any of them. Whether Gyrocopter's death was worth its own gold — that is outcome reasoning, banned in chapter 01, and it does not stop being banned because the number is impressive.

The record can price staying. It can never price leaving. One side of the exit decision is in the file — gold, experience, a building, a body — and the other side is a counterfactual that no replay in existence contains.

Which is why I should have left sooner feels like a lesson and teaches nothing. It compares what happened against an imagined alternative whose price you have never once calculated. You are not comparing two options. You are comparing a real outcome against a fantasy in which leaving was free, instant and successful — and this fight contains three demonstrations that it is none of those.

So the decision breaks into four parts, and only the first is about the item:

PartThe questionWhy it is the hard one
The exitWhat specifically removes you from here? Easy, and the only part most players have. "Run" is not an answer at fifty minutes
The priceWhat does using it cost beyond the item? Your share of the fight. Here that share was up to 1,769 gold and 5,912 experience
The expiryAfter what event is it no longer available? Almost nobody writes this down, and it is the one that decides the outcome
The triggerWhat will you see that means now? Without it you leave on feeling, which is to say too late or not at all

Note what this chapter is not doing, because the neighbours own it. Chapter 03 traces how an escape gets closed — the sequence, before this moment. Chapter 17 owns cooldowns as a clock in their own right; here a cooldown is only part of the exit's price. And chapter 20 is about the cost of declining a fight you have not entered, which is a different decision with a different price: this chapter begins with you already inside one.

Where this stops applying. Not every hero has an exit, and the honest version of this chapter says so. Some heroes are built to arrive and not to leave; for them the whole procedure collapses into pricing the engagement beforehand, which is where it belongs anyway.

The gold and experience deltas price the fight for the players who were in it, under professional coordination, on a map where two barracks were already down. They do not establish what a fight is worth in your game, and nothing here should be read as a target.

And the failure rate observed here is three of three, from one fight. That is an illustration that exits fail, not a measurement of how often they do. If this chapter ever makes you refuse to use an exit because "they do not work", it has done harm; the point is to price it, not to distrust it.

What is owed. Anything about what leaving costs. The counterfactual is the centre of this chapter and it is absent from every record available to it — no replay contains the game in which somebody left. The nearest thing to evidence would be a bracket-stratified sample of fights entered and abandoned, and the public match sample behind this book carries duration, rank tier and result and no fight data of any kind, which is a registered claim. So the chapter teaches the pricing procedure and quotes no price.

Next game

  1. Before you take a fight, name your exit out loud — the specific item, spell or route. If the honest answer is that you have none, say "no exit". That is the single most useful sentence in this chapter and it takes half a second.
  2. With the exit, name its expiry: the thing after which you will not have it. Their stun, their initiator arriving, your mana going under, the cooldown you are about to spend on something else.
  3. Name the trigger — one observable event that means use it now. One, not a list. You will not check a list at fifty minutes.
  4. When you use an exit and it fails, note that it failed. Not as a mistake — as a data point. Exits have a failure rate and you are entitled to know yours.
  5. Once a game, when you decide to stay, say what staying is buying. If you cannot name it in five words, that is the finding, and it is more useful than the fight.

After the game

Use a fight where you said the words — exit, expiry, trigger — because they take half a second and this loop needs them. Without them the honest result is not enough evidence to reconstruct my process, which is a real outcome; a known ending will happily invent a plan you never had.

Open the replay at the start of that fight, not at your death.

The observable decision: the moment your stated trigger occurred — and whether anything happened when it did.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. You had named an exit, an expiry and a trigger before engaging, and when the trigger fired you either used the exit or made a fresh decision not to. Staying deliberately passes. Staying because you had stopped looking does not.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. Given what you could see at the time: was that exit realistic, and was the trigger early enough to be actionable? A trigger of when I am about to die is a fully specified plan and a useless one. This asks whether the plan was worth having, not whether the fight went well.

Exit: force staff, then the trees behind me Price: my part of this fight, and the staff for the next one Expires: when their initiator blinks, or when I am below half Trigger: the moment their carry turns toward me

Four results, as everywhere: good process and good calibration; good process and poor calibration — the plan was followed and was not worth following; no process, so nothing to grade; or not enough evidence. Whether you survived goes on its own line and never moves the grade — three heroes in this chapter's fight survived nothing despite doing everything, and one gained more than anybody by dying.

Ten games of this and you will have something no guide can give you: your own exit failure rate, and a sense of whether your triggers fire early enough to be worth naming.
