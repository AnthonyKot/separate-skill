Part I · Chapter 04 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

Leaving Is a Skill

The fight is going wrong and you know it. There is a moment when the thought arrives: I should get out of here.

And then you do not, and afterwards the lesson you draw is that you should have left sooner. That lesson is nearly useless, because it is not a decision. It is a wish. The decision has four parts and none of them is leave: the mechanism that removes you, what using it costs, the event after which it is gone, and the earlier signal that says use it now.

You are not being asked whether to fight. You are in one. Commit in writing:

  1. What is your exit? Name the specific thing that removes you — not "run".
  2. What does using it cost? The item and its cooldown are the small part. Name the rest: your share of the fight, the position, what your team is left holding.
  3. When does it expire? The observable event after which that exit is no longer available to you. Without this written down, the plan is incomplete.
  4. What is the trigger? The thing you will see that means: use it now.

The honest answer may be that you cannot do this from what is above — and that is the fifth admissible response, not a failure. The second panel lists what the record withholds, and naming an exit without a position or a health bar is guesswork dressed as method. If you take that route, say which missing item blocks you. Chapters 01 and 02 spent themselves establishing that discipline and this chapter does not get to suspend it.

Two more answers are admissible that people assume are not. I have no exit, and I priced that before engaging is complete — many heroes have none, and knowing it beforehand is worth more than wishing for one afterwards. Note that it does not mean I must die here: it may mean the engagement was mispriced, or that the remaining decision is how to lose less.

And I intend to spend my life here, and here is what it buys — but with a condition, because otherwise it validates anything. Naming a purchase does not make the exchange sound. The purchase has to have been plausibly available from what you knew at the time, and zero deaths remains a valid maximum cost. A player can always invent something a death allegedly bought; that is the ex-post error from chapter 01 wearing a noble expression.

What does not pass is an exit with no expiry, because an exit you have not dated is a feeling.

What happened next

Undying used the scroll. Undying used both blinks. Undying died at 50:15, twenty-six seconds into the fight.

Doom did the same arithmetic with a different inventory — scroll, blink, Shiva's — and died at 50:04, fifteen seconds in. Earthshaker used a blink and a force staff and died at 50:21. Dark Seer used two blinks and a wind waker and lived.

Here is what the record will and will not support, and the difference is the chapter.

It will not support "three exits failed." A town portal scroll can bring a player into a fight. A blink is an entry tool at least as often as an escape. The file holds no destination and no intention, so nothing above establishes that anybody tried to leave.

What it does hold is where everybody stopped. All seven deaths are recorded with map positions, and they fall inside 20.6 cells of one another — a single pile, in the parser's own grid. Then the figure this chapter turns on:

The hero who spent the most mobility died nearer the centre of the pile than five of the other six. That is not evidence that his exit failed, because it is not evidence that he was leaving. It is something narrower and harder to argue with: spending mobility did not, here, put anybody anywhere else.

Which is the first half of this chapter. An item in your inventory is not an exit. Mobility is a resource an exit consumes, and it can be consumed for four other purposes first — and once it is gone it cannot be counted again at the moment you finally decide to go.

Gyrocopter died in this fight, and recorded its largest gains — more gold and more experience than anyone on the map, including Dark Seer, who survived it.

Read that carefully, because the tempting reading is not available. It does not show that staying produced those gains, that leaving earlier would have forfeited them, or that the death bought anything. Some of it may have been banked before any exit decision arose. What it shows is one thing and it is enough: survival and value are two different measurements, and in this fight they came apart completely.

The reasoning

Observed. The fight window and its seven deaths with timestamps; the two conflicting death counts; every item use inside the fight; every gold and experience delta; the aegis at 47:39; the barracks before and the tower at 51:12.

Supported inference. That an exit is not free and not certain. Three heroes spent seven movement-item uses inside forty-seven seconds and all three died — whatever those uses were for, they did not produce survival.

Hypothesis, and a weak one. That any of those items was used in order to leave. A scroll can bring a player in as easily as take one out. A blink is an entry tool at least as often as an escape. The record holds no destination and no intention, so every use of the word exit in this chapter describes a category of item, never a proven purpose. The argument does not need it: it needs only that the items were spent and the deaths happened.

Unknowable. Whether anybody tried to leave, should have left, or should have left earlier. What leaving would have cost any of them. Whether Gyrocopter's death was worth its own gold — that is outcome reasoning, banned in chapter 01, and it does not stop being banned because the number is impressive.

The record can describe the path taken. It cannot price the path forgone. One side of the exit decision leaves a trace — gold, experience, a position, a body — and the other is a counterfactual that no replay in existence contains.

Which is why I should have left sooner feels like a lesson and teaches nothing. It compares what happened against an imagined alternative whose price you have never once calculated. You are not comparing two options. You are comparing a real outcome against a fantasy in which leaving was free, instant and successful — and this fight contains three demonstrations that it is none of those.

So the decision breaks into four parts, and only the first is about the item:

PartThe questionWhy it is the hard one
The exitWhat specifically removes you from here? The easy part. "Run" is not a mechanism
The priceWhat does using it cost beyond the item? Your share of the fight. The 1,769 gold and 5,912 experience above is an upper bound on what missing the whole fight could forfeit — never the marginal cost of leaving at one particular second, which is the number you actually need and cannot have
The expiryAfter what event is it no longer available? The part most often left implicit — and an implicit expiry cannot be checked against
The triggerWhat will you see that means now? Without it you leave on feeling, which is a decision made by whatever you happen to notice

The trigger must come before the expiry, and if they are the same event the plan is broken. Leave when their initiator jumps me names the moment the exit stops existing and calls it the moment to use it. In the example below, the initiator blinking is the expiry and the enemy carry turning toward you is the trigger — one is earlier than the other, and that gap is the entire plan.

Note what this chapter is not doing, because the neighbours own it. Chapter 03 traces how an escape gets closed — the sequence, before this moment. Chapter 17 owns cooldowns as a clock in their own right; here a cooldown is only part of the exit's price. And chapter 20 is about the cost of declining a fight you have not entered, which is a different decision with a different price: this chapter begins with you already inside one.

Where this stops applying. Not every hero has an exit, and the honest version of this chapter says so. Some heroes are built to arrive and not to leave; for them the whole procedure collapses into pricing the engagement beforehand, which is where it belongs anyway.

The gold and experience deltas price the fight for the players who were in it, under professional coordination, on a map where two barracks were already down. They do not establish what a fight is worth in your game, and nothing here should be read as a target.

And the failure rate observed here is three of three, from one fight. That is an illustration that exits fail, not a measurement of how often they do. If this chapter ever makes you refuse to use an exit because "they do not work", it has done harm; the point is to price it, not to distrust it.

What is owed. Anything about what leaving costs. The counterfactual is the centre of this chapter and it is absent from every record available to it — no replay contains the game in which somebody left. The nearest thing to evidence would be a bracket-stratified sample of fights entered and abandoned, and the public match sample behind this book carries duration, rank tier and result and no fight data of any kind, which is a registered claim. So the chapter teaches the pricing procedure and quotes no price.

Next game

  1. Before you take a fight, name your exit out loud — the specific item, spell or route. If the honest answer is that you have none, say "no exit". That is the single most useful sentence in this chapter and it takes half a second.
  2. With the exit, name its expiry: the thing after which you will not have it. Their stun, their initiator arriving, your mana going under, the cooldown you are about to spend on something else.
  3. Name the trigger — one observable event that means use it now. One, not a list: a list is not checkable mid-fight.
  4. When you use an exit and still die, note it. Not as a mistake — as a data point about whether your triggers fire early enough to leave you anything to use them on.
  5. Once a game, when you decide to stay, say what staying is buying. If you cannot name it in five words, that is the finding, and it is more useful than the fight.

After the game

Use a fight where you said the words — exit, expiry, trigger — because they take half a second and this loop needs them. Without them the honest result is not enough evidence to reconstruct my process, which is a real outcome; a known ending will happily invent a plan you never had.

Open the replay at the start of that fight, not at your death.

The observable decision: the moment your stated trigger occurred — and whether anything happened when it did.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. You had named an exit, an expiry and a trigger before engaging, and when the trigger fired you either used the exit or made a fresh decision not to. Staying deliberately passes. Staying because you had stopped looking does not.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. Given what you could see at the time: was that exit realistic, and was the trigger early enough to be actionable? A trigger of when I am about to die is a fully specified plan and a useless one. This asks whether the plan was worth having, not whether the fight went well.

Exit: force staff, then the trees behind me Price: my part of this fight, and the staff for the next one Expires: when their initiator blinks, or when I am below half Trigger: the moment their carry turns toward me

Four results, as everywhere: good process and good calibration; good process and poor calibration — the plan was followed and was not worth following; no process, so nothing to grade; or not enough evidence. Whether you survived goes on its own line and never moves the grade — three heroes in this chapter's fight used every mobility action the record shows them using and died inside the same pile as everyone else, and a fourth died while recording the fight's largest gains.

Ten games of this and you will have something no guide can give you: a record of how often your trigger fired at all, and whether it fired early enough to leave anything to decide.
