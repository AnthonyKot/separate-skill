Part I · Chapter 01 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

The Death You Didn't Notice

The game ends. You finished 3/9/12. Somebody types that you fed, and you close the client with the vague sense that they are right and the specific sense that you do not know which nine deaths they mean.

So you do the responsible thing. You open your match history, look at the number nine, and resolve to die less. You have now performed the entire ritual of self-improvement available to you, and you have learned nothing at all.

What actually happened

Not to you. To ten professionals, in a game with every event recorded.

Team resilience vs PlayTime · The Games of the Future 2026 · match 8928953683 · patch 7.41e

Forty-nine minutes and thirty-three seconds. Fifty-nine hero deaths. The scoreboard at the end contained, among others, these two lines:

Bane0 / 12
Storm Spirit18 / 1
Hero deaths, total59
Match length49:33

Twelve deaths and no kills. One death and eighteen kills. If death count measured anything on its own, those two lines would be the whole story of the game, and you would already know which of the two played well.

Here is the figure that makes the scoreboard useless rather than merely crude. Of those fifty-nine deaths, thirty happened outside every window the parser recognises as a teamfight. Slightly more than half of all the dying in a professional game happened when there was, by that measure, no fight going on.

Stop here and commit before reading on

Below are three deaths from that match. Everything shown is in the record. Nothing else about them is in the record — that is not a summary, it is the complete contents.

A 8:15 Nature's Prophet killed Mirana no teamfight window no building fell within 45s B 14:24 Earth Spirit killed Mirana no teamfight window a building fell within 45s C 20:36 Earth Spirit killed Storm Spirit no teamfight window no building fell within 45s (this hero died once in 49 minutes)

For each of the three, write four things down before reading on:

  1. Your provisional classification. Necessary risk, execution failure, free risk — or unclassifiable from this record.
  2. How confident you are in that classification.
  3. What would have had to be gained for the death to be acceptable.
  4. The single missing fact most likely to change your answer.

Unclassifiable is a permitted answer and not a way out of a hard one. Do not choose it because the judgement is difficult. Choose it only if you think the record genuinely cannot support a judgement at all.

What happened next

None of the three can be classified responsibly from that record. If you marked any of them unclassifiable, you passed the first test in this book: recognising when the available evidence does not support a decision.

For these three, the record contains no position, no vision state, no cooldowns and no health — a time, a victim and a killer, and nothing else.

An earlier version of this chapter said that more broadly, and it was wrong. It claimed the record holds no positions and no vision state at all. It holds both. Thirty of the fifty-nine deaths in this match carry map coordinates, and the number of observer wards standing at any moment can be reconstructed exactly — there were three at 14:24. The correction is kept visible rather than quietly edited in, because a book that claims to check its sources should show what happens when one of its own claims fails.

And the shape of the error is the argument, which is why it improves the chapter rather than damaging it. Coordinates exist for deaths inside a teamfight window and not for deaths outside one. The kills log puts 29 deaths inside fights and 30 outside; the record carries positions for thirty. The two structures disagree by one, which is its own small demonstration that "teamfight" is the parser's judgement rather than the game's.

So the record sees the deaths that happened in fights, and does not see the deaths that happened alone — and it is the second group that this chapter is about, the second group that is half of all dying, and the second group you have the least help diagnosing. All three deaths above are from it. That is not a coincidence; it is the only place the ambiguity lives.

So the honest reading of every line above is: a hero died, and something you cannot see decided whether that was correct.

Death C is the one worth sitting with. It is the only death that hero suffered in forty-nine minutes, which makes it almost invisible in the final score. The record gives no basis for deciding whether it was a catastrophic mistake, a justified trade, or neither — and the temptation to read something into its rarity is worth noticing, because that is the same move the scoreboard makes. A single death is reported as a 1. What the 1 contained is not a question the scoreboard is asking.

The reasoning

Being strict about the evidence, because this chapter is about what a number does not contain:

Observed. Fifty-nine hero deaths; the time, victim and killer of each; the scoreboard lines; the match length.

Supported inference. Death totals do not distinguish between kinds of death, because the record that produces them contains nothing about circumstance.

Hypothesis. That the thirty deaths outside fight windows are, on average, more avoidable than the twenty-nine inside them. Plausible — nobody is trading in a coordinated exchange when no exchange is happening — and unestablished. Note also whose definition is doing the work: "teamfight" is the parser's judgement, not the game's, and a death at the edge of a window is a classification artefact.

Unknowable. Whether any individual death in this match was correct. What anyone intended, saw, or had available.

So here is the framework the rest of Part I runs on. Every death you take belongs to one of three classes, and they need completely different repairs.

ClassWhat happenedWhat fixes it
Necessary riskYou accepted the risk, a plausible exchange was available, and you took itNothing. This is the cost of playing
Execution failureThe decision was sound; the hands, the timing or the click were notPractice, not analysis. This book will not help
Free riskYou accepted substantial risk with no plausible exchange availableChapters 02, 03 and 04

The definition that separates the third from the first is deliberately awkward, and the awkwardness is the point:

A death is free when, given what was reasonably knowable at the time, you accepted substantial death risk without a plausible exchange — in objectives, information, resources, space, time, or another player's survival.

Given what was reasonably knowable at the time. Not given what happened. A support who dies buying six seconds for a core has made a correct decision whether or not the core converted those six seconds; a player who walks into the dark and survives because nobody happened to be there has made a bad one. The exchange has to have been available, not successful.

This is the distinction the scoreboard cannot represent and the reason the number nine told you nothing. Twelve deaths on a position-five hero may be twelve correct purchases of space. One death on a mid may be the game. Neither figure is evidence about anything until you know what was available at the moment the risk was taken — and the only place that information exists is the replay.

Which is the uncomfortable conclusion of the first chapter of a book about decisions: you cannot do this from the scoreboard, and there is no shortcut that lets you skip watching yourself die. What the rest of Part I does is make that watching efficient. Chapter 02 is the information that was on your screen before it happened. Chapter 03 is the thirty seconds that removed your exit. Chapter 04 asks what concrete exit remained, what using it would have cost, and what signal should have triggered it. Chapter 05 is the deaths you should have taken and did not, because a chapter this pleased with itself about avoidable deaths will otherwise teach you to avoid all of them, which loses games in a quieter way.

Where this stops applying. The three classes are not equally useful to everyone. If your deaths are mostly execution failures — you decided correctly and the hands did not follow — then classifying them will be an entertaining way to avoid the practice that would actually help, and this book is not your first book. The exclusion is stated at the front for that reason.

The framework also assumes you can reconstruct what was knowable. In a game you barely remember, played four days ago, you cannot, and a classification made from memory is fiction. Classify from the replay or do not classify.

What is owed here. An inversion — a case where classifying deaths carefully made a player worse. The plausible shape: a player who becomes so attentive to avoidable deaths that they stop taking necessary risks, which chapter 05 exists to prevent but which no case in this book yet demonstrates.

Also owed: any evidence at all about how often each class occurs at Archon–Legend. This chapter establishes that the classes exist and that the scoreboard cannot apply them. It says nothing about which class is most common in your games, and no bracket sample in this book is registered yet.

What transfers, and what does not. This is a professional match used to demonstrate a limitation of a data source. That is an unusually safe use of one — the argument would survive if the players were far worse, because the missing fields are missing for everybody.

Transfers: the three classes; the ex-ante test; the fact that a death count is not a measurement of anything on its own; that roughly half the deaths in a game can happen with no fight in progress.

Does not transfer: any judgement about these ten players, which is why none of them is named and why only heroes appear above. A scoreboard line of 0 and 12 is not an accusation, here or in your own game. It is a prompt to go and look.

Next game

  1. After the game, write down the number of times you died. Then close the scoreboard. That number is the last useful thing it will give you.
  2. Open the replay and find three of your deaths. Not all of them — three.
  3. For each, write the class before you write the excuse: necessary risk, execution failure, or free risk.
  4. For any you call free, write the exchange that was available and that you did not take. If you cannot name one, it was not free — it was something else, and you have learned something.
  5. Do this for one game a week. Not every game. One.

After the game

Open your last loss and pick your second death. Not the first, which you will rationalise, and not the last, which you already remember.

The observable decision: the moment you committed to the position you died in — the click that put you there, not the one that killed you.

The pass mark, set before you look: you can state, in one sentence, the exchange that was available at that moment, and whether you took it. Pass if you can name it, whether or not you got it. Fail if the honest sentence is I was not thinking about an exchange.

Then one more question, which is the one that stops this becoming a formality: was that exchange worth the risk you accepted for it? Naming an exchange is not the same as having found a good one. I went in to reveal their position and it cost me a death and a buyback is a stated exchange, and it may still have been a poor trade on what you knew at the time. That judgement is made from the same information you had then — it is not a question about how the fight turned out.

So the review lands in one of four places, and only the first two are about your decision: you named an exchange and it was worth the risk; you named one and it was not — the most useful result this exercise produces, and the one a single question cannot see; you named none, so there is nothing here to grade; or you cannot now reconstruct what you knew, which is an honest answer and the same one the hold above offers you.

Note what is not being graded. Not whether you died — you are reviewing a death, so you did. Not whether the exchange came off, which depended on four other people. Only whether there was one, and whether you knew it at the time.

One replay, one death, one sentence. Three to six games a week does not give you enough repetitions to learn this by volume, so this is where the repetitions come from.

Chapter 01 is a pilot, testing whether the diagnostic method avoids hindsight. The hold offers unclassifiable as one of its options deliberately: the skill being tested is not whether you can classify three deaths, but whether you notice that the evidence does not permit it. That distinction was added after review, because the first version asked for three classifications and then announced they were impossible — which tested compliance rather than judgement.
