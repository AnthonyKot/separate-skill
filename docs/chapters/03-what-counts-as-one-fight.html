Part I · Chapter 03 · Can you distinguish free deaths from necessary risk?

What Counts as One Fight

You lost a fight and you can describe it. They caught our support, we went in to help, it collapsed, we lost four. One sentence, one event, one cause, and it explains everything.

The trouble is that you assembled it. What you were actually given was a stretch of time containing some deaths, and the joining-up — we went in to help — is yours. This chapter is about the step before tracing what removed your escape: establishing that the thing you are tracing happened once.

What actually happened

Gaimin Gladiators vs Nigma Galaxy · The Games of the Future 2026 · match 8928851109 · patch 7.41e

Forty-seven minutes and two seconds; Radiant won. The parser records six teamfights. Here is the third of them, exactly as the record hands it over — a window, a label, and a list.

Four deaths against two, inside a minute. That is a rout, and a rout has an obvious story: one side got caught, the other side punished it, and the punishment ran until there was nobody left to punish. You can feel the sentence forming.

And a familiar disagreement. The fight's own deaths field reads 4 while the kill log inside the window gives six. The same undercount appeared in chapter 04's case, from the same parser. Both figures are registered here. The label on a thing and the contents of a thing are different objects, which is going to turn out to be the whole chapter.

Stop here and commit before reading on

You have the six deaths and their times, and nothing else. That is genuinely all the record offers before the next section. Commit in writing:

  1. Which of these six deaths belong to the same engagement? Draw the lines. It is legitimate to answer all of them, and it is legitimate to split them — if you split, say where and say why.
  2. From this list, what — if anything — can you establish about what removed the escape of the last hero to die at 19:30? Nothing, from this record is a valid and possibly correct answer; if you give it, say what you would need.
  3. What single further fact would most change your answer?

The timings are the only evidence you have and they are enough to reason from. There is an eleven-second gap between the second death and the third, and a fifteen-second gap between the fourth and the last two. Whether gaps like those mean anything is exactly what you are being asked.

These are one engagement and here is why the pauses do not trouble me is admissible. So is a split at either gap, or at both. What is not admissible is a sequence with no joins stated, because an unstated join is the thing this chapter is about.

Two marks, as everywhere in this book, and they are separate here for a reason. The process mark is whether you stated a boundary and the evidence you were using for it. The calibration review is whether the further evidence supported that boundary, weakened it, or left it unresolved. You can state a boundary well and have picked the wrong one, and the next section is going to produce exactly that result for one of the two available splits.

What happened next

Every one of those six deaths carries a map position, and the positions are not close together.

Puck and Techies died 1.0 cell apart. Everyone else died between 63 and 69 cells away from them, and within 26 cells of each other. The full spread of this "one teamfight" is 69.6 cells.

For comparison, chapter 04's fight — seven deaths in forty-seven seconds — had a spread of 20.6: one tight cluster. This window holds two spatially distinct clusters of deaths.

And that is all it holds. Two clusters of deaths are not two engagements, and this chapter is not going to say they are. They could be one fight that crossed the map, a pursuit, two fronts of one commitment, two unrelated skirmishes, or one decision followed by another. The record has no movement in it, so it cannot distinguish any of those — a limit stated here rather than three paragraphs later, because an earlier version of this page called them "two events that share a label" and that was a claim the evidence does not carry.

Where the split comes from, stated so you can argue with it. The grouping uses a 30-cell threshold, and that number is this book's choice rather than the record's. It happens not to matter here: any threshold between roughly 30 and 60 produces the same two groups, because the gap between them is 63 cells wide and the widest gap inside either group is 26. A case where the answer moved with the threshold would not be usable, and this chapter would have said so.

So the eleven-second pause is where the positions change. And now the part that matters more than the split, because it is the opposite of what the timings appeared to say.

The longest gap in the sequence is fifteen seconds, and it falls inside one of the clusters. The place where the positions actually change is the eleven-second gap — the second-longest. A reader who split at the biggest pause, which is the obvious heuristic and the one this chapter nearly taught, would have split in the wrong place.

So the honest lesson is narrower than trust the gaps, and more useful: a pause is a place to look, not a boundary. The eleven seconds gave you a candidate. The coordinates are what made it worth something — and they are also what demoted the fifteen. Timing tells you where to check. It cannot segment the sequence by itself.

The reasoning

Observed. The window and its label; six deaths with times; six positions; the distances between them; the parser's own death count of four against the log's six.

Supported inference. That this window contains at least two spatially distinct clusters of deaths, separated by eleven seconds and by 63 to 69 cells, when the widest gap inside either cluster is 26. Nothing more: not two engagements, not two decisions, not two fights.

Hypothesis. That anything in the first group caused anything in the second. Attractive — a support dies, the team responds, the response fails — and entirely unestablished. The record holds no movement, so there is nothing connecting the two groups except that they fell inside one window somebody drew.

Unknowable. Whether any of the six could have escaped. What removed anyone's escape. What anybody did between 18:54 and 19:05, which is the eleven seconds the whole causal story would have to live in.

Before you can trace what removed your escape, you have to establish that it was one thing that removed it. A window is a label. Whether it contains one event is a question, and the genre routinely answers it by assumption — as does memory.

And note that events and decisions are not the same count, which is where this could go wrong in the other direction. One engagement can contain four fresh commitments. Two engagements can both follow from a single decision taken before either. A player can cross the map and never decide anything. So the order is four steps and only the last is about pricing:

  1. Segment the observable sequence — what is in the window, when, and where.
  2. Locate the candidate joins — the pauses and the position changes.
  3. At each join, ask whether you recommitted, or arrived without deciding.
  4. Price each commitment separately. That is chapter 04's job, and it needs this one done first.

Which is why this chapter is shorter on prescription than its neighbours, and why it sits where it does. Chapter 02 asks what you could have known before you moved. Chapter 04 asks what your exit was and what using it would have cost. This one asks the question that has to be answered before either of those can be applied to a stretch of a game: how many decisions are in front of me?

And the practical failure it prevents is specific. When you review a loss as one event, you look for one cause, and you will find one, because a sufficiently motivated reviewer always does. Here the available causes are things that happened sixty-odd cells away from each other with an eleven-second hole in the middle — and any story joining them is written by you, from material the record does not contain.

Where this stops applying. Plenty of windows really are one fight. Chapter 04's is: seven deaths inside twenty-one cells, and splitting it would be inventing a distinction the record does not support. The move is to check, not to assume fragmentation any more than to assume unity.

The grid is not game units and this chapter converts nothing. Every distance here is comparative — this fight against that one, this group against that group — and a reader who turns 63 cells into a number of metres has left what the record supports.

And you do not have coordinates in your own review. What you have is a replay you can scrub, which is better: you can watch the eleven seconds instead of reasoning about them. The procedure below is built for that, not for this.

Which raises the obvious question about this chapter's own case, and the answer is unflattering. Forty seconds of tape would probably settle whether those two clusters were one engagement — did anyone cross the map, was there a break in contact, did the second group begin as a response. This book has not watched it. Its sourcing standard declares direct observation as a class of evidence and has never once used it: there is no client here and no way to open a replay file. So this chapter stops where the structured record stops, and the reader with a replay open can get further than the book can.

What is owed. Any claim about how often a labelled window contains more than one engagement — here, or at any bracket. This is one window in one professional game. The bracket sample behind this book carries duration, rank tier and result and no fight data of any kind, which is itself a registered claim, so there is no evidence about frequency and the chapter makes none. What would settle it is a sample of fights with death positions across a stated bracket, which nothing available supplies.

Next game

  1. When a fight ends badly, note the clock twice: when it started for you, and when you died. Two numbers, said out loud during the respawn timer.
  2. Say where you were at each. Not coordinates — a landmark. Their tier two, our triangle, the river outside Roshan.
  3. If those two places are far apart, you have learned that the sequence changed location — not that "the fight moved", which already assumes it was one fight. The next question is whether the engagement moved, ended and restarted, or became something else.
  4. Once a game, before you explain a loss to yourself, ask how many separate things am I explaining? If the answer is one, say what makes it one.
  5. Do not automatically fold in every death that was near yours in time. The kill feed tells you one happened, not where; in the replay you can check whether it belonged to the same local sequence, and often it did not.

After the game

Use a fight where you noted the two clocks. Without them the honest result is not enough evidence to reconstruct my process — and this chapter is the reason that matters, because a replay will happily show you one continuous stretch of time and leave the segmenting to you.

Open the replay at the first clock, not the second, and watch forward.

The observable decision: the moment you committed to the second place — which is a different decision from the one you made at the first, and the one you probably never reviewed.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. You can state where the sequence began, where you died, and — the observable part — the action that committed you to the second place: a teleport, a rotation, a spell, crossing a piece of terrain, continuing a chase. Pass if you can name that action. If you cannot reconstruct whether it was deliberate, mark insufficient evidence; a replay shows movement and cannot show a decision.

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. Given what was visible when you committed to the second place: was that a fight worth joining on its own terms? Not should I have helped — whether the second engagement, priced by itself, was worth what it cost. A commitment inherited from an earlier decision has not been priced; it has been continued.

Began: 18:53, top rune, I was not there Died: 19:30, our bottom triangle Joined at: 19:05 — I moved when I saw the first death on the minimap Priced?: no. I moved because we were losing, not because of what I could add

Four results, as everywhere in this book: good process and good calibration; good process and poor calibration — you decided at the join and decided badly; no process, meaning there was no decision at the join at all; or not enough evidence. Whether the fight was lost goes on its own line and never moves the grade.

Ten of these gives you a count within the ten you chose — and you will have chosen the memorable and the ambiguous ones, so it is not your rate at anything. It is a starting sample about how often you can find a committing action at all, which is not available anywhere else and which no scoreboard has a column for.
