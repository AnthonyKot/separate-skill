Part III · Chapter 12 · Can you turn resources into capability?

Every Item Is a Forecast

You have four and a half thousand gold and a shop open. You know what you want. You have wanted it since the draft, and you have been counting toward it for six minutes.

There is a different question, and it takes about four seconds to ask: what is this for, and roughly when? Not a timestamp — nobody can produce one. A named event or a bounded window: before the next Roshan, before their carrier finishes the thing that ends me, during the push we have been building toward. If the honest answer is eventually, in a fight, that is worth knowing before you spend rather than after.

What actually happened

BoomBoys vs OG · 1win Essence II · match 8928916055 · patch 7.41e

Forty-one minutes and fourteen seconds, and — unusually — four teamfights in the entire game, as the parser counts them. The second ended at 10:08. The third did not begin until 33:07.

That is a stretch of 22 minutes and 59 seconds containing no fight the parser recognises as a teamfight. The game was busy: seven buildings were destroyed inside that window, and sixteen expensive items were completed.

Stop here and commit before reading on

It is 21:00. You are Largo, on the winning side of a game whose last teamfight ended eleven minutes ago. Everything below is in the record.

YOU Largo, net worth 11,188 — second highest in the game THEM Kez · Monkey King · Night Stalker · Enchantress · Windranger CLOCK last teamfight ended 10:08 — eleven minutes ago BUILDINGS three have fallen since then, two of theirs, one of yours GOLD enough for one expensive item now, or a bigger one later

This is not a test of whether you can guess the professional's item. It is a test of whether your purchase has a function, a forecast and a cost. Commit in writing:

  1. The function you buy — not the item, the job it does.
  2. The window in which you expect that function to first matter. A named event or a bounded stretch, not a timestamp and not "a fight".
  3. The acceptable cost — what you give up by committing the gold now rather than holding it.
  4. What would invalidate the forecast between now and then.

If your honest answer to the second question is whenever the next fight happens, write that down too. It is a real answer, and the rest of the chapter is about what it costs.

Five players, across both teams, completed the same item within four minutes and twenty-two seconds. The next parser-recognised teamfight was at 33:07 — seven minutes and forty seconds after the last of the five, and twelve minutes after the first.

Stated carefully, because the temptation here is to claim more than the record holds: the fight eventually came, and spell immunity is a function suited to it. The record cannot tell us whether completing the item that early was optimal, whether it had already changed the game by making its holder harder to punish, or whether any of the five was thinking about that particular fight. What it does show is that the purchase and its clearest visible use were separated by many minutes.

And note what "no teamfight" does not mean. Seven buildings fell in that window. An item can matter long before it is switched on — by letting someone hold a position, contest a tower, or farm somewhere they otherwise could not. None of that is necessarily visible in a structured record, which is a limit of the evidence rather than a finding about the game.

The reasoning

Observed. The four fight windows; the 22:59 stretch; the five completion times and the 4:22 spread; sixteen expensive completions; seven buildings.

Supported inference. An expensive item can be completed a long way from its first observable use — here, between eight and twelve minutes.

Hypothesis. That the five purchases were made for the fight at 33:07. Plausible and weak. Nothing in a replay connects a purchase to an intention, and five players converging on one item is at least as easily explained by five players reading the same draft.

Unknowable. Whether any of these purchases was correct; what any alternative would have produced; what any of them intended.

So this chapter cannot tell you what to buy, and would be lying if it tried. What it can give you is the shape of the decision:

Every expensive item purchase is a forecast. You are predicting which function the game will demand of you, roughly when it will demand it, what you can safely surrender by committing the gold now, and what new information would prove you wrong.

An item's value is not that it occupies a slot. Its value is the action it makes possible at the moment the game asks for one — and because that moment is in the future, buying is forecasting whether you admit it or not. The only question is whether the forecast is stated or implied.

Which is why is this item good on my hero is the wrong question without being a stupid one. It has an answer, the answer is usually yes, and it fails to discriminate between the purchase that wins and the purchase that sits in a slot for ten minutes. The discriminating questions are the other three:

The question everyone asksThe questions that decide it
Is this item good on my hero?What function does this game demand of me?
Is it worth the gold?In what window will it first matter?
What do the good players build?What am I surrendering by committing now, and what would change my mind?

Both answers to the second question are legitimate, which is the part the genre gets wrong. Committing early buys certainty about a future you have predicted. Holding buys flexibility about one you have not. Those five held finished items for eight to twelve minutes before their clearest visible use, which at the professional level is affordable and may well be right. The cost is real in both directions: gold converted into an item has stopped being able to become a different item, and that matters exactly when the game surprises you.

Where this stops applying. This is not an argument for buying late. A team that arrives at a fight without the function that fight demands has made a worse error than one that arrives eight minutes early, and nothing in this record suggests the five were wrong.

It does not apply to cheap items or consumables, where the four seconds are not worth spending. It applies to the purchase costing multiple minutes of your income.

And it assumes the window is forecastable at all. In a game where the next fight genuinely cannot be anticipated, buying for flexibility is the correct answer rather than a hedge.

What this case does not establish. How Archon–Legend players buy items, when they buy them, or whether copying professional timings hurts them. The intended argument — that a professional buys early because their team will produce the moment, while a solo-queue player buying on the same clock is buying toward a moment nobody has scheduled — is reasoning rather than evidence, and it stays out of the chapter until a source with bracket-stratified item timings is registered. The source note below says why none is available.

What transfers, and what does not.

Transfers: the forecast framing; the four things a purchase commits you to; the observation that an item can be finished long before its first observable use.

Does not transfer: the eight-to-twelve minute lead time, and every item timing in this match. Those five bought early against a fight they had reason to expect. The second question — in what window — is precisely the one a solo-queue player answers with less confidence, which changes the answer rather than the procedure.

Next game

  1. Before your next expensive purchase, say the function out loud — the job, not the item.
  2. Name the window it first matters in: an event, or a bounded stretch you could point at.
  3. If you cannot name one, do not complete the purchase on reflex. Choose deliberately between holding the gold, finishing a component you want either way, and buying a cheaper function that matters immediately. All three are decisions; only the reflex is not.
  4. Reassess when the state changes rather than on a timer: an objective comes up, an enemy item is revealed, a major cooldown returns, or your team moves somewhere else.
  5. Do this for one purchase per game. The biggest one.

After the game

Open the replay at the moment your biggest item completed.

The observable decision: the purchase, and what you could have known when you made it.

Two marks, and they are separate on purpose.

1 · The process mark, set before you look. Before buying, you could name the function, the window, what you were surrendering, and what would change your mind. Then check one thing separately — did that invalidating information appear, and did you respond to it?

2 · The calibration review, which the process mark cannot do. On what you knew at the time: was that function the one the game was going to demand of you, was the window plausible, and was what you surrendered a fair price for it? This is not asking how the purchase turned out. It is asking whether the forecast was a reasonable forecast to make — and without it the first mark passes anyone who filled in four blanks, however badly.

Four possible results, and the second is the one worth having: good process, good calibration; good process, poor calibration — you named all four things and the forecast was not a sensible one; no process, so nothing to grade; not enough evidence to reconstruct what you knew. Whether the item actually mattered is recorded on its own line, below, and never moves the grade.

Function: keep acting while they cast at me Window: the fight over their tier-two, next two or three minutes Surrendering: the component that would have answered their carrier instead Changes it: they take Roshan, or their mid finishes first

Record the item's first observable use as a separate line, not as a grade. A forecast that missed by six minutes is calibration data, and after ten games you will know whether you habitually buy early, late, or with no window in mind at all. What it is not is a verdict on the decision, because a good forecast can fail and a bad one can be rescued by luck.
